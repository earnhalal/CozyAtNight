How to Choose an Electric Blanket: Safety, Size, Controls and Running Costs

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · This is a buying guide — no product is recommended or sponsored. General guidance only; always follow the manufacturer instructions for the model you buy.

Learning how to choose an electric blanket starts with safety, not softness. A blanket is a heating appliance you sleep on or under, so the checks that matter come first: certification, overheat protection and automatic shut-off. Pass those, then compare size, controls and running costs.

This guide works through those checks in order, so you can rule out unsuitable options quickly.

How to choose an electric blanket, step by step: start with safety

Safety is the filter that removes most options. Three features are non-negotiable, and if a listing cannot confirm all three clearly, move on.

Look for a recognised safety certification or listing

Choose a blanket that carries a recognised safety certification or listing mark for your country or region — the mark showing it was assessed against local electrical safety standards. Buy from a reputable seller, and check that the plug, voltage and instructions match your country's supply.

Check for overheat protection

Overheat protection senses when part of the blanket gets too hot — for example if it is folded or bunched — and cuts the power. A blanket must always lie flat while switched on, and concentrated heat in a fold is exactly what this system prevents, so treat it as essential.

Insist on auto shut-off with a timer

An automatic shut-off turns the blanket off after a set period, and a timer lets you choose that period. Together they mean the blanket cannot run at full heat all night because you fell asleep. Many people pre-warm on a higher setting, then use a low setting or the timer for sleeping. A model with no shut-off at all is a model to leave behind.

Check who should not use one

Always follow the manufacturer guidance in the manual. As a general pattern, some manufacturers advise against use for infants and young children, for people who cannot operate the controls safely, and for people who may not be able to react to overheating. Some models also carry specific advice for people with certain medical conditions or fitted medical devices. If any of that could apply in your household, read that section first, and ask a medical professional if you are unsure.

Underblanket or overblanket: choose the type that fits how you sleep

An underblanket — often fitted, like a mattress protector — goes on the mattress, under your sheet, and warms the bed from below. It stays in place, is usually left in position for the season, and suits people who want the bed warm when they get in. Lying under a sheet also protects it from spills and wear.

An overblanket goes on top of you, over a sheet or in place of a lighter layer, and moves between rooms more easily. A heated throw is the smaller, living-room cousin, made for a sofa or chair rather than a full bed. If you are weighing those two, read our guide to the difference between a heated throw and an electric blanket before you decide, because their sizes and jobs are not the same.

Size and fit: match the mattress, not the duvet

Buy for the mattress the blanket will sit on. Single or twin sizes fit a single bed, double or full fits a double, and queen and king sizes fit their matching larger mattresses. Too small leaves cold edges; too large cannot lie flat — and a blanket that cannot lie flat should not be switched on.

Fitted underblankets with elasticated skirts or straps stay smooth and suit restless sleepers. Check mattress depth as well as width and length — deeper mattresses need a deeper skirt. Tie-down styles can work too, as long as they stay taut with no folds or bunched corners.

Controls: the part you will use in the dark, half asleep

Controls decide how a blanket feels in daily use, so think about the moment you will use them: in a dark room, half asleep. Look for controls that are easy to read and reach from bed, with buttons you can work by feel and a clear display of the setting.

The number of heat settings matters less than the gap between them — a genuinely low setting is more useful for sleeping than several similar medium ones. Dual controls, with a separate control and heating zone for each side, are the upgrade couples value most, because two sleepers rarely want the same warmth. If you share a bed and disagree about temperature, put dual controls high on your list.

Useful extras include a timer in simple steps, a pre-heat or fast-heat setting that warms the bed quickly before you lower it, and detachable controls — needed anyway for washing, below. Also check where the cable runs; a control pressing into your shoulder or a cable crossing a walkway will annoy you every night.

Materials and feel: comfort you notice, warmth you do not

Finishes are usually described in general terms such as fleece, microfibre or cotton-style fabrics. Fleece-style finishes feel warm to the touch straight away, smoother microfibre feels lighter and can be easier to wash and dry, and cotton-style finishes feel more like ordinary bedding. None changes how much heat the blanket makes — the elements do that — but the finish changes how warm the bed feels when you get in.

What matters more is even construction: the blanket should feel consistent, with no lumpy sections, and lie flat without stiff ridges. If you are a warm sleeper, you can also turn the room down — our guide to the bedroom temperature range that helps most people sleep in winter explains why a cooler room with a warmer bed often beats heating both.

Washability and care: a blanket is a seasonal appliance

Most modern blankets are washable only because their controllers detach, so confirm that before you buy, and follow the care label exactly — wash method, temperature and drying differ between models. Never switch a blanket on to dry it, and make sure it is completely dry before use or storage.

Store it flat, or loosely rolled, never tightly folded with sharp creases, and keep heavy objects off it so the wiring is not crushed. At the start of every season, lay it out and inspect it for fraying, scorch marks, exposed or kinked wires, and damage to the cable or controller. If you find damage, stop using it. Follow the manufacturer's age guidance — blankets are not lifetime appliances.

Running costs: small wattage, simple maths

Electric blankets are among the cheapest heating appliances to run because they warm you directly. Typical models use about 50–150W. The formula is the same one we use throughout this site: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A 100W blanket is 0.1kW, so at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh it costs about 1.6 cents per hour, and at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh about 2.4p per hour — roughly 13 cents or 19p for eight hours, and less on a low setting.

Those rates are clearly labelled examples, not promises: check your own bill for your real price per kWh. For the full side-by-side numbers, see our electric blanket versus space heater running-cost comparison. The honest rule is unchanged: a blanket saves money when it lets you turn your main heating down for the night.

Frequently asked questions

What should I look for when buying an electric blanket?

Start with safety: a recognised safety certification or listing for your country, overheat protection and an auto shut-off timer. Then check that the size matches your mattress, that the controls suit you — dual controls for couples sharing a bed — that the controllers detach for washing, and that the wattage suits your needs.

Are electric blankets safe?

A modern, undamaged electric blanket used exactly as its manufacturer directs is generally considered safe for healthy adults. Choose one with a recognised safety certification or listing, overheat protection and auto shut-off, inspect it each season for fraying, scorch marks or damage, never use it folded or bunched while switched on.

What size electric blanket do I need?

Match the blanket to your mattress size: single or twin for a single bed, double or full for a double, and queen or king for larger beds. A fitted underblanket should be sized for the width and depth of your mattress so it lies completely flat, with no folding or bunching.

How much does an electric blanket cost to run?

Use this formula: kilowatts multiplied by your price per kilowatt-hour. A typical blanket uses 50–150W, so a 100W model is 0.1kW. At an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh that is about 1.6 cents per hour; at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh it is about 2.4p per hour. On a low setting, the real figure is usually lower.

How often should you replace an electric blanket?

Follow the age guidance in your model's manual, and replace the blanket sooner if you find fraying, scorch marks, exposed wires, a damaged cable or controller, or uneven heating. Inspect it at the start of every season, and do not keep using a blanket that is damaged or well past the manufacturer's stated lifespan.
