10 Draught Fixes Under $25 That Actually Make a Room Feel Warmer

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — prices are typical examples that vary by shop and country; not a substitute for professional safety advice.

The most effective draught fixes are unglamorous: a strip under a door, tape around a frame, a cover over a keyhole. A quick language note: a draught (British spelling) and a draft (American spelling) are the same thing — an unwanted current of cold air — and draft proofing is simply stopping it. You are not imagining that icy line at ankle height.

None of the ten fixes below costs more than $25 in materials per door, window or fireplace — most cost far less. Prices are typical examples only — they vary by shop and country — so treat them as a budgeting guide, not a quote. Used together with the habits in our guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up, they are often the difference between a room that never quite warms up and one that holds its heat.

How to find draughts before you spend anything

Spend nothing until you know where the air is moving. On a cold, breezy day, start with the back of your hand: hold it near — not on — door edges, window frames, letterboxes, keyholes, skirting boards, floorboard gaps and the loft hatch. Moving air feels distinctly colder against skin, and the strongest leaks announce themselves immediately.

For smaller gaps, hold tissue or lightweight paper near the edge: a flutter means air is passing. A lit candle or incense stick also works — watch the flame or smoke waver — but keep it away from curtains and anything flammable, never leave it unattended, and prefer tissue near soft furnishings. Write the gaps down, biggest first — that list is your shopping list.

Draught fixes under $25: the 10 worth doing first

Each fix is priced per job — one door, one window, one fireplace. Work in the order of your own gap list.

1. Door brush strip or door sweep

A brush strip or rubber sweep screws or sticks to the bottom of a door and closes the gap to the floor or threshold. Typical material cost: roughly $8–$20. Measure, cut to size, and leave the brush just touching the floor so the door still opens freely. Usually the single biggest win on an external door.

2. Self-adhesive foam or V-strip tape

Foam tape and springy V-strip are the classic draft proofing materials for door sides and tops, and for sash or casement windows. Typical material cost: roughly $5–$12 per roll, enough for one or two openings. Clean the frame first, then apply it so it compresses slightly when closed — flat, it seals nothing; too thick, and the door will not shut.

3. Weighted door snake or draft excluder

The simplest draft excluder of all: a fabric tube, weighted or foam-filled, laid against the bottom of an internal door. Typical cost: roughly $8–$20 to buy, or almost nothing made from old fabric and filling. It needs no fitting and suits renters, but only works while in place — and does nothing for gaps around the frame.

4. Letterbox seal or mail slot cover

A letterbox (UK) or mail slot (US) is a hole in your front door, and cold air pours through it. A brush or flap seal fitted inside typically costs roughly $8–$18 and takes minutes to screw on. Measure the slot before ordering — sizes vary — and check post still slides through.

5. Keyhole cover

An uncovered keyhole in an external door is a tiny wind tunnel, and on a breezy night you can feel it from a metre away. A swinging escutcheon cover typically costs roughly $3–$8 and fits with one or two screws. It is the cheapest fix on this list, and oddly satisfying: one small disc of metal, one draught gone.

6. Window insulation film

Clear shrink film, fixed over a window frame with double-sided tape and tightened with warm air from a hairdryer, traps a still layer of air against single glazing. Typical material cost: roughly $8–$20 for a kit covering several windows. A winter-only, renter-friendly fix — but it must come off before you open the window in spring.

7. Sealant or caulk for fixed gaps

For gaps that never move — around frames, along skirting boards, around pipe entries — flexible sealant or decorator's caulk is the right tool. Typical material cost: roughly $5–$12 per tube. Run a smooth bead, tidy it with a damp finger, and let it cure. Never use it on a moving edge, where tape belongs instead.

8. Chimney balloon or damper for an unused fireplace

An open, unused chimney is a wide shaft pulling warm air straight out of the room. An inflatable chimney balloon or a fitted damper blocks that shaft above the fireplace opening, typically for roughly $15–$25. Leave a note visible so nobody lights a fire beneath it, and never fit one to a flue in use.

9. Radiator reflector foil

Not strictly a draught fix, but a natural partner: foil panels behind a radiator on an outside wall bounce heat back into the room instead of warming the brickwork. Typical material cost: roughly $10–$20 per roll, enough for several radiators. The panel simply slides down behind the radiator and is taped in place. It pairs well with the radiator habits in our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night.

10. Loft hatch seal

The loft or attic hatch is a door into the coldest space in the house, often completely unsealed. Foam tape around the hatch frame, plus a simple latch pulling it down snugly, typically costs roughly $5–$15 in materials. Check any insulation on top of the hatch is still intact.

The safety line: what you must never seal

Draft proofing means sealing unintentional gaps — never purpose-made ventilation. Homes need fresh air, and some openings exist to keep you safe. Never block boiler or furnace vents, air bricks, trickle vents, or any flue. A boiler, furnace, gas fire or other fuel-burning appliance that cannot draw air and expel fumes properly can produce carbon monoxide, an odourless gas that can be fatal. Keep a working carbon monoxide alarm in any room with a fuel-burning appliance, If unsure whether an opening is ventilation or just a gap, leave it alone and ask a qualified heating engineer.

Ventilation matters for moisture, too: a sealed home traps steam from cooking, showers and laundry, which condenses on cold walls and windows. Seal the gaps, then keep using extractor fans, open a window briefly after a shower, and leave trickle vents open. Warm and dry beats warm and damp, every time. If a draught comes from damage — a warped door or cracked frame — tape is a sticking plaster; get the fault fixed.

What order should you do these in?

  1. Biggest felt gap first — usually under the front or back door, or around the worst window.
  2. External openings next — letterbox, keyhole, unused chimney, loft hatch.
  3. Fixed cracks after that — sealant around frames, skirting boards and pipe entries.
  4. Comfort extras last — radiator foil, heavy curtains, rugs over floorboard gaps.

That principle — keep heat in before you make more of it — also runs through our comparison of the electric blanket vs space heater running costs: sealed rooms let smaller, cheaper heat sources do their job properly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to stop draughts?

Start with the biggest, easiest gaps: a weighted door snake or a brush strip under a door, plus self-adhesive foam tape around a window or door frame. Each typically costs only a few dollars in materials, needs no tools beyond scissors, and tackles the gaps you can feel. Work room by room, biggest gap first, rather than buying everything at once.

Do draught excluders really work?

Yes, for the gap they are fitted to. A brush strip, foam seal or door snake physically blocks moving air at the bottom of a door or around a frame, so the cold stream you could feel is stopped or greatly reduced. They do not add heat, so a room still needs a heat source — they simply stop warmth leaking away so quickly.

How do I find where a draught is coming from?

On a cold, windy day, run the back of your hand slowly around door and window frames, letterboxes, keyholes, floorboard edges and loft hatches — moving air feels colder against skin. A strip of tissue or lightweight paper held near a suspect gap will flutter in a draught. Check one room at a time and note each gap before you buy anything.

Can draught-proofing cause condensation?

It can contribute if a home becomes under-ventilated, because moisture from cooking, showers and drying clothes then has fewer ways to escape. Seal unintentional gaps, but never block purpose-made ventilation such as air bricks, trickle vents or boiler flues, and keep using extractor fans and opening windows after steamy activities. If condensation or mould worsens, improve ventilation rather than sealing more gaps.

What is the difference between a draught and a draft?

There is no difference in meaning — it is purely spelling. Draught is the British spelling for a current of cold air (and for draft proofing in the UK), while draft is the American spelling used in the US and Canada. This guide uses draught, but every fix here works exactly the same whichever word you search for.
