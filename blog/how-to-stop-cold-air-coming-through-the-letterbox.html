How to Stop Cold Air Coming Through the Letterbox: Brushes, Flaps and Keyhole Fixes

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. If your front door is a fire door — common in flats — do not alter it or its fittings without checking the building's fire-safety requirements.

Knowing how to stop cold air coming through the letterbox matters because a letterbox is not a small leak — it is a slot cut right through your front door, and on a windy night it can pour more cold air into the hall than every window frame in the house combined. The good news: it is also one of the cheapest draughts to fix properly, with options for owners and renters, and every good fix still lets the post through. This guide covers the internal brush, the cover flap, the letterbox cage, and the often-forgotten keyhole beside it.

The letterbox is one stop on a bigger tour — our guide to how to stop a draught coming through the front door works through the bottom gap, the frame edges and the threshold too, and our checklist for how to find draughts in your home will show you whether the letterbox or the door edges are your bigger leak.

First, find where the air actually gets in

Hold the back of your hand near the letterbox on a cold, windy day, or use the tissue test from our draught-finding guide. Air comes through a letterbox in two ways: through the flap itself, when its spring is weak or it no longer closes flat, and around the edges of the plate, where the cut in the door is slightly larger than the fitting or old sealant has failed. The fixes differ — a tired flap wants a brush or cover; a leaky perimeter wants sealing — so spend two minutes identifying yours before buying anything.

Fix one: the internal brush strip

The classic answer is a brush strip fitted on the inside of the door, over the letterbox opening. Dense bristles let letters and packets slide through, then spring back to block the airflow almost completely. Fitting is normally two or four screws into the door, or strong adhesive pads on some models — measure your letterbox opening first, because sizes vary, and make sure the brush covers the slot fully with a margin around it. Brushes cope with daily post, do not rattle, and are the fix most people should start with. If your door is a fire door — many flat front doors are — check before drilling or replacing fittings: fire doors and their hardware are part of the building's fire protection, and unapproved changes can compromise both.

Fix two: an internal cover flap or draught excluder plate

A solid or brush-backed cover on the inside — sometimes called an internal letterbox flap or draught excluder — adds a second barrier behind the external flap, so cold air has to defeat two doors instead of one. Some are magnetic or gravity flaps; some are simple hinged covers you lift when expecting a parcel. They work best combined with a brush, and they have a second benefit: security and privacy, since the slot can no longer be peered or reached through. Whatever you fit, test it with the actual post: a fix that stops letters arriving will be removed within the week, and a removed fix stops nothing.

Fix three: a letterbox cage or basket

A cage or basket mounted inside the door catches the post — and, incidentally, breaks up the stream of cold air so it no longer jets straight into the hall. On its own it is the weakest of the three fixes for draughts, because it does not seal the slot; combined with a brush strip, it is the complete job: sealed slot, caught post, no letters scattered across a cold floor. Magazine racks sold for the same purpose work the same way. Measure the swing and the space behind the door before fitting, so the cage does not foul the wall or floor when the door opens fully.

Do not forget the keyhole

Beside the letterbox sits its smaller accomplice: an uncovered keyhole is a tiny, perfectly round wind tunnel, and on a still hand-test it often surprises people. A simple keyhole cover — an escutcheon with a swinging or sprung cover — costs very little, fits in minutes and closes that hole completely while still letting the key in. While you are at the door, run a hand around the frame edges too; our draught fixes under $25 guide covers the foam tape and brush strips those edges want. Letterbox, keyhole and edges together turn the front door from the hall's biggest leak into a sealed part of the wall — and you will feel the difference the same evening.

Renters and the no-drill options

If you cannot drill into the door, adhesive-fitted brush strips and magnetic internal covers exist and remove cleanly at the end of a tenancy; keep the original fittings if you replace anything. A freestanding letter cage that hooks over or stands behind the door avoids fixings altogether. What not to do: tape the letterbox shut. It blocks your post, annoys the person delivering it, and on a fire door can be a genuine problem. The draught is fixable without defeating the door's actual job.

How to stop cold air coming through the letterbox: seal the plate itself

One leak hides behind the others: the gap between the letterbox plate and the door. The slot is cut slightly oversize, plates warp a fraction with age, and old sealant crumbles — leaving a thin perimeter draught that no brush can fix, because it bypasses the brush entirely. Run the tissue test slowly around the plate's edges to find it. The cure is to remove the plate if it comes off easily, clean the mating surfaces, and refit it on a fresh bead of suitable flexible sealant; if it stays put, a careful bead run around the outside edge achieves most of the same. Let it cure fully before judging the result on the next windy night.

Then verify the whole door as a system. Letterbox sealed, keyhole covered, edges stripped — hold the tissue at each point in turn on a windy evening and watch for stillness. This is the same methodical check as the checklist in how to find draughts in your home, and it is worth the five minutes: a front door is several separate products fitted into one hole, and each settles and shrinks at its own rate. Seal them one by one and the hallway's temperature on a cold morning is the receipt.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to stop a draught through a letterbox?

An internal brush strip fitted over the opening on the inside of the door is the classic fix: bristles let post through, then spring back to block the airflow. Adding an internal cover flap as a second barrier, and sealing any gap around the letterbox plate, completes the job.

Will a letterbox brush still let the post through?

Yes — that is what it is designed for. The bristles flex as letters and small packets pass, then close again. Measure your letterbox opening before buying, fit the brush with a margin around the slot, and test with real post so nothing snags.

Can I just tape over my letterbox to stop the draught?

Please do not. Tape blocks your post, usually fails within days, and on a fire door can compromise fire protection. Brush strips, internal covers and adhesive-fitted options all stop the draught while keeping the letterbox working as intended.

Why does cold air come through my keyhole too?

An uncovered keyhole is a small open tube straight through the door, and wind pressure pushes air through it surprisingly strongly. A keyhole cover — an escutcheon with a swinging or sprung flap — costs very little, fits in minutes and closes the hole while still admitting the key.

Can I draught-proof a letterbox on a fire door?

Only carefully, and with permission. Many flat front doors are fire doors whose fittings are part of the building's fire protection. Check with the building owner or manager before drilling, replacing or covering any fitting, and use only hardware approved for fire doors.
