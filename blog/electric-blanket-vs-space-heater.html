Electric Blanket vs Space Heater: Which Is Cheaper to Run?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

Put an electric blanket vs a space heater side by side on a winter night and the running-cost contest is not close: the blanket wins by a mile. A typical blanket sips power in the tens of watts, while a typical heater pulls well over a thousand. But there is a catch, and it is the same one behind our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night: the two are not really doing the same job. One warms a person. The other warms a room.

Below we work through the real numbers — wattage, cost per hour and per night at clearly labelled example rates, when each one is the right tool, and the safety rules that matter more than either bill.

Electric blanket vs space heater at a glance

The short version: an electric blanket typically uses 50–150 watts and warms you, in bed, for hours. A space heater typically uses 750–1,500 watts and warms the air in a room, usually while people are awake in it. Because running cost is power × time × your price per kilowatt-hour, that wattage gap — roughly 10 to 15 times — flows straight through to the bill.

How many watts does an electric blanket use — and how many does a space heater use?

Start with the label on the box, because wattage is the whole story here. A single or double electric blanket usually draws between 50 and 150 watts depending on size and heat setting, and some larger models go up to around 200 watts. On a low overnight setting, many draw less than their maximum.

A plug-in space heater is a different class of appliance. Small ceramic and fan heaters commonly run at 750 to 1,500 watts, and many UK fan heaters are rated up to 2,000 watts on their highest setting. Heating a room's worth of air genuinely takes that much energy — so one hour of a heater at full power uses roughly the electricity a 100-watt blanket uses in ten to fifteen hours. Same socket, same price per unit, completely different bill.

How much does an electric blanket cost to run — per hour, per night, per winter?

The formula never changes: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A 100-watt blanket is 0.1 kilowatts. A 1,500-watt heater is 1.5 kilowatts. Multiply each by an example electricity price and the gap becomes money.

Worked example — two clearly illustrative rates. We will use 16 US cents per kWh as an example US residential rate, and 24p per kWh as an example UK price-cap-style rate. Neither is a promise about your bill: US rates vary widely by state and UK tariffs change with the price cap and your deal. Use the rates printed on your own bill for your real answer — the method is identical.

Electric blanket, 100W: 0.1 kW × 16¢ = about 1.6 cents per hour in the US example, or 0.1 × 24p = about 2.4p per hour in the UK example. Run it for a full 8-hour night and the electric blanket cost per night is roughly 13 cents, or about 19p. Even run every night for a 30-night month, that is under $4, or under £6.

Space heater, 1,500W: 1.5 kW × 16¢ = 24 cents per hour in the US example, or 1.5 × 24p = 36p per hour in the UK example. That space heater cost per hour looks modest — until you multiply by a night: eight hours is about $1.92, roughly $2, or about £2.88. Every night for a month, around $58, or around £86. A 2,000W UK fan heater on full power would cost more again.

Two honest qualifications. A heater with a thermostat cycles once a room is warm, so a real night may cost less than the full-power figure — and a blanket on a low setting costs less than its maximum too. Neither changes the order of finish: at any tariff, the blanket costs a small fraction of the heater.

The honest catch: is it cheaper to use an electric blanket than heating — or are they just different jobs?

Here is the part most cheap-heat headlines skip. Is it cheaper to use an electric blanket than heating a room? Yes — but mainly because it is doing less. A blanket delivers warmth straight into a bed, with almost no waste. A heater takes on the far bigger task of raising and holding the temperature of all the air in a room, plus its walls and furniture, including heat that drifts to the ceiling and out of the windows.

That difference decides who should use which. In bed, or settled in one spot, warming the room around you pays for warmth nobody is sitting in. Up and moving — getting dressed, working at a desk, protecting pipes or holding damp at bay — a blanket does nothing for you, and a heater (or your main heating) is the right tool. Neither is “better”. They solve different problems at very different prices.

And one rule matters more than the comparison itself: a blanket only saves money if it lets you turn something else down. The winning pattern is blanket on, thermostat (or bedroom radiator) turned lower for the night. Run a blanket on top of a fully heated house and you have simply added its cost to the same bill — and a space heater adds a much bigger one. As we put it in our full guide to heating a room at night for less: warm the person first, the room second, the house last.

Can you leave an electric blanket on all night?

For many modern blankets, yes — with conditions. Blankets sold today typically include overheat protection and an automatic shut-off timer, and many are designed to run on a low setting through the night. The pattern most manufacturers recommend is simple: switch the blanket on higher 30–60 minutes before bed, then turn it down to low, or off, for sleeping. Stored warmth plus a good duvet is often enough for the whole night.

The conditions are not optional. Never use a blanket that is folded or bunched up while switched on, because concentrated heat damages elements. Inspect the blanket, cable and controller at the start of every season; scorch marks, fraying or a damaged cord mean it should be replaced, not repaired at home. Follow your model's age guidance — blankets are not lifetime appliances — and its instructions about who should not use one. When in doubt, pre-warm and switch off.

Is it safe to sleep with a space heater on?

This is where the heater loses on safety as well as cost. The general advice from fire services is not to leave a space heater running unsupervised — and sleeping counts as unsupervised. A heater needs clear space around it, away from bedding, curtains, clothing and furniture, and a sleeping household cannot guarantee that space stays clear all night. It should also be plugged directly into a wall socket, not an extension lead.

If a room genuinely must stay heated overnight — for someone unwell, or to protect pipes in extreme cold — choose a model with tip-over and overheat protection, on a flat, clear surface, and follow its instructions to the letter. An oil-filled radiator, with no exposed hot element, is often the calmer overnight choice. But for a healthy adult who simply feels cold in bed, warming the bed itself is both safer and dramatically cheaper.

So, which is cheaper to run? At the socket, it is the blanket, and it is not close — roughly ten to fifteen times cheaper per hour at typical wattages, pennies per night instead of pounds or dollars. Which should you run tonight depends on the job. In bed: pre-warm with the blanket, turn the house down, sleep warm for pennies. Awake and moving: run the heater in bursts, door closed — and switch it off at bedtime. Used that way, the two become a team: the heater handles the evening, the blanket handles the night.

Frequently asked questions

How much does an electric blanket cost to run per night?

At the illustrative rates used above, a typical 100W blanket costs about 1.6 cents (US) or 2.4p (UK) per hour — roughly 13 cents or 19p for an 8-hour night. Your real figure is kilowatts × your price per kWh × hours, and on a low setting it usually comes in under these examples.

Is it cheaper to use an electric blanket than heating a room?

Yes, by a wide margin while it warms you in bed: it uses roughly a tenth of a heater's power and loses almost no heat to the room. The saving is only real if it lets you turn your main heating down — on top of full heating, it saves nothing.

Can you leave an electric blanket on all night?

Many modern blankets are designed for it on a low setting, with automatic shut-off and overheat protection, but only do what your model's instructions explicitly allow. The recommended pattern is to pre-warm the bed, then switch to low or off. Never run a blanket that is damaged, very old, or folded while switched on.

Is it safe to sleep with a space heater on?

Generally, it is not recommended to leave one running unsupervised all night. If overnight heat is genuinely essential, use a model with tip-over and overheat protection, keep it clear of bedding and curtains, plug it straight into the wall, and follow the manufacturer's guidance. For most people, the blanket is the safer — and far cheaper — overnight answer.

How many watts does an electric blanket use compared with a space heater?

A typical electric blanket uses about 50–150 watts (some models reach 200W); a typical space heater uses 750–1,500 watts, UK fan heaters up to 2,000W. That 10–15× power difference is why the running costs are so far apart.
