How to Stop Condensation on Bedroom Windows in Winter?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — not a substitute for professional glazing, building or health advice.

If you want to know how to stop condensation on bedroom windows in winter, start with the pattern, not the puddle on the sill. The glass is wettest first thing, worse after a cold night, and worse again when laundry is drying indoors. That tells you the water comes from everyday life inside the home, and the window is simply where it shows up.

Most bedroom condensation responds to unglamorous habits done consistently: wipe the glass dry, change the air briefly, let air move around the room, keep some gentle background heat, and make less moisture in the first place. No gadget purchase is needed to begin.

Where all that moisture comes from

A bedroom fills with moisture while you sleep. Every breath out releases water vapour, and with one or two people — sometimes a pet — breathing in a closed room for seven or eight hours, that adds up night after night, even though the vapour itself is invisible.

The rest of the home contributes too. Showers send steam drifting through hallways unless the bathroom door stays shut and an extractor fan or open window deals with it at source. Cooking — especially pans boiling without lids — releases steam that does not stay in the kitchen. Drying laundry indoors is one of the biggest sources of all: a load releases the water it holds into the air around the airer, hour by hour, as it dries. In winter, when windows stay shut, all of that moisture has fewer chances to leave.

Why the water lands on the glass

Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air. When warm, moist air touches a cold surface, the thin layer of air against it cools, can no longer hold all its moisture, and deposits the excess as liquid water. That is condensation, and it always chooses the coldest available surface first.

On a winter night, window glass is usually that surface — colder than the surrounding wall, and colder still by morning, when heating has been lower and hours of breathing have loaded the air. Frames, the bottom corners of panes and cold wall patches behind furniture are other common spots. Water also runs down and collects on the sill, which is why wiping there matters: water left sitting day after day is what damages paintwork and encourages mould along the seal.

How to stop condensation on bedroom windows in winter: the morning routine

The most effective habit is a short morning routine. First, wipe the glass and sill dry with a cloth or squeegee. It takes a minute or two, and it physically removes the night's moisture instead of letting it evaporate back into the air to condense again the next night. Wring the cloth into a sink rather than leaving it wet in the room.

Second, change the air. Open the window wide for a short, deliberate period — a brief, thorough airing rather than a narrow crack left all day. Cold winter air usually holds less moisture, so as the fresh air warms up indoors, the room's relative humidity drops. If your windows have trickle vents, leave them open: they are purpose-made ventilation, designed to let a home breathe slowly without a draught. As our draught fixes guide puts it — seal unintentional gaps, but never block purpose-made ventilation. Leaving the bedroom door open for a while after getting up helps air circulate through the home, too.

Let air move: curtains, blinds and furniture

Still, trapped air against cold glass is condensation's best friend. Heavy curtains drawn tight to the window, or blinds flush to the glass, create a pocket of cold, still air where moisture condenses readily and dries slowly — which is why mould so often starts hidden behind a curtain. Leave a gap between curtains or blinds and the glass so room air can circulate, and open curtains fully during the day. If curtains pool over a radiator under the window, they can also funnel warm air up against the cold glass instead of into the room.

Furniture follows the same logic. A wardrobe or bedhead pushed hard against a cold external wall traps still, moist air behind it, and that hidden patch can become colder and damper than the rest of the room. Pull large pieces a few centimetres away from external walls so air can move and any problem stays visible.

Keep some consistent background heat

Surfaces allowed to get very cold collect condensation even in a reasonably dry room. Bedrooms heated in short, fierce bursts and then left unheated overnight often suffer more than rooms kept at a steadier, modest temperature, because consistent background heat keeps glass and walls a little warmer — and a warmer surface needs more humid air before water forms on it.

This does not mean heating a bedroom to living-room temperatures all night, which would be expensive and uncomfortable for sleep. It means avoiding extremes where practical, and remembering that warmth works as a set with ventilation and moisture control: heating hard while trapping moisture indoors simply gives that moisture more warm air to ride in.

Managing humidity, and making less moisture at source

A useful general target for indoor relative humidity in winter is roughly 30 to 50 percent. Consistently above that range, condensation becomes more likely; well below it, air can feel uncomfortably dry. A small hygrometer can help you learn your bedroom's pattern, but treat any single reading as a guide, not a verdict. If your home sits at the dry end instead, our guide to adding moisture to the air without a humidifier covers the opposite problem — the balance point between too dry and too wet is this same middle ground.

Lowering moisture at source is the cheapest lever. Put lids on pans and use the extractor fan, or a briefly opened window, while steam is being produced rather than afterwards. In the bathroom, run the extractor during a shower and for a while after, keep the door shut so steam is dealt with in one room, and wipe down screens and tiles — water removed on a cloth never enters the air. Avoid drying clothes in the bedroom where possible: choose a better-ventilated room, keep its door shut, open a window slightly or use an extractor, and give clothes an extra spin first so they hold less water. When the water is between the panes

Everything above deals with condensation on the room side of the glass — the surface you can wipe. Condensation between the two panes of a double-glazed window is different: it usually means the sealed unit has failed, its edge seal has broken down, and moist air is trapped inside the unit where no cloth or ventilation can reach it. A failed unit also insulates less well, so that window may run colder than its neighbours.

Replacing or repairing a sealed unit is a professional glazing job, so get a glazier's assessment rather than attempting to drill or seal the unit yourself. If you rent, report it to your landlord or letting agent in writing — window units are normally part of the property's fabric, not the tenant's responsibility.

Mould around windows: clean carefully, escalate sensibly

Where condensation sits day after day, mould can follow, usually as small dark spots along sealant, frames and corners. For small areas, careful cleaning following local guidance and the instructions on any product you use is a reasonable first step: ventilate the room, protect your hands, avoid dry-brushing mould around the room, never mix cleaning products, and dry the area thoroughly afterwards.

Cleaning alone does not fix the cause, so treat it as one job with the moisture control above. Large or spreading patches, mould that keeps returning despite better ventilation and heating, or any situation where you have health worries should be treated as a professional or landlord matter — raise it promptly and keep a simple record of what you have tried. This guide does not offer medical advice; if you are concerned about your health, speak to a qualified medical professional.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my bedroom windows have condensation every morning in winter?

Because warm, moist air from breathing overnight — plus steam from showers, cooking and drying laundry elsewhere in the home — meets glass that has cooled overnight. The air next to the glass cannot hold as much moisture, so water appears as droplets on the coldest surface in the room.

Should I open bedroom windows in winter to stop condensation?

A brief opening helps. After wiping the glass dry in the morning, opening a window wide for a short period, or using trickle vents if you have them, swaps moist indoor air for drier outdoor air. It is usually more effective than leaving a window on a tiny crack all day, which can cool the room without clearing much moisture.

What humidity level should a bedroom be in winter?

A common general target is roughly 30 to 50 percent relative humidity indoors in winter. Consistently above that range makes condensation more likely, while much lower can feel uncomfortably dry. A simple hygrometer can help you see the pattern, but the trend matters more than any single reading.

What does condensation between double-glazed panes mean?

Moisture trapped between the two panes usually means the sealed unit has failed and its insulating seal is no longer airtight. Wiping and ventilating will not clear it, because the water is inside the unit itself. Replacing or repairing a sealed unit is a professional glazing job.

When should I worry about mould around bedroom windows?

Small areas can be cleaned carefully following local guidance and the instructions on any product you use, with the room ventilated. Large, spreading or persistent mould, mould that keeps returning after you have improved ventilation, or any situation where you have health worries should be treated as a professional or landlord matter rather than a DIY cleaning job.
