How Much Does It Cost to Run an Oil Filled Radiator Per Hour?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

If you are asking how much does it cost to run an oil filled radiator per hour, the short answer is that it depends on one number printed on the label: its wattage. At an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, a small 700W model costs about 16.8p per hour at full power, a mid-sized 1,500W model about 36p, and a large 2,500W model about 60p. Once the thermostat starts cycling, the real average usually falls below those full-power figures.

Below: typical sizes, costs per evening and per eight-hour night, why the thermostat matters, and where an oil radiator earns its place — alongside the other options in our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night, compared room by room.

How much does it cost to run an oil filled radiator per hour at different wattages?

The formula is the same for every electric heater: cost per hour = kilowatts × price per kWh. To turn watts into kilowatts, divide by 1,000. A 1,500W radiator is 1.5 kW, so at 24p per kWh it costs 1.5 × 24p = 36p for each hour it runs at full power.

Illustrative rates, clearly labelled. This article uses 24p per kWh as an illustrative UK rate and 16 US cents per kWh as an illustrative US rate. Neither is your tariff. UK prices change with the price cap and your deal, and US prices vary widely by state. Find the unit rate on your own bill and use the same formula — the method does not change.

UK oil filled radiators commonly range from about 700W for a small model to about 2,500W for a large one. A lower setting costs less per hour but puts less heat into the room, so it may run longer. Here is the full-power picture at the illustrative UK rate:

For US readers, the same formula at the illustrative 16¢ per kWh gives about 11.2¢ per hour for 700W, 24¢ for 1,500W and 40¢ for 2,500W at full power. The wattages and the method are identical; only the unit price changes.

The thermostat and duty cycle: why full power is rarely the real cost

An oil filled radiator does not normally run its element flat out all evening. You set a thermostat, the element heats the oil until the room reaches roughly that temperature, and then the element switches off. As the room cools slightly, it switches back on. That on-off pattern is called the duty cycle.

The arithmetic is simple and worth understanding. If a 1,500W radiator is only actually drawing power for, say, half of each hour once the room is warm — a purely illustrative example, not a promise — its average draw is about 750W, and its average cost at 24p per kWh is about 18p per hour instead of 36p. If it cycles for a third of the time, the average falls to roughly 12p per hour. Your real cycle depends on the size of the room, how cold it is outside, draughts, insulation and the temperature you choose.

Two lessons follow. A bigger radiator is not automatically dearer to live with: a 2,000W model may reach temperature then cycle gently, while a 700W model runs non-stop and still feels weak. And the thermostat setting is your real cost control — every step down shortens the on-time. Sizing is covered in our guide to how to choose a space heater for your room size and needs.

Heat that lingers: warmth after switch-off

The oil is the point of the design: the element heats it, it heats the metal fins, and the fins warm the room. Because oil and metal hold heat, the radiator keeps giving off warmth after switch-off, cooling gradually instead of going cold at once.

That lingering heat is not extra energy from nowhere — it is heat you already paid for, released slowly. What it changes is comfort: the temperature glides down instead of dropping, which suits winding-down evenings and bedrooms. Many people switch off a little before bed and let the stored warmth finish the job.

Oil filled radiator vs fan heater: different behaviour, same price per unit

It is tempting to ask which type is cheaper, but at equal wattage the honest answer is neither: a kilowatt-hour costs what your tariff says, whichever appliance uses it. A 2,000W fan heater and a 2,000W oil radiator both cost 48p per hour at full power at 24p per kWh. Their behaviour, though, is opposite.

A fan heater warms air fast — you feel it within minutes, which suits a quick burst while getting dressed or starting work — and the moment it goes off, the warmth and the noise stop. An oil radiator starts slowly, then settles into quiet, even heat with no fan, no blowing dust and no cold patch when it cycles off.

So the two suit different jobs: short, sharp warming favours the fan heater; long, steady background warmth favours the oil radiator. Our sibling guide on how much a halogen heater costs to run per hour covers another common option with the same formula.

When an oil filled radiator suits a bedroom — and when it does not

Bedrooms are where oil radiators make their strongest case. They are near-silent, with no fan blowing air and dust around, their heat is gentle and even rather than a hot blast, and residual warmth smooths the first part of the night. A home office used all day benefits the same way.

They are weaker elsewhere. If you need a room warmed quickly — a bathroom before a shower, a spare room for a guest in twenty minutes — the slow warm-up works against you. In a large or draughty room a small model may never reach its setting, running at full power continuously while still feeling inadequate. They are also heavy, and stay hot, so they make poor carry-from-room-to-room heaters.

The pattern that works best: start earlier than you think, on a moderate setting, door closed, and let the thermostat hold the temperature. That makes the overnight cost the cycled figure, not the ceiling.

Safety: the rules that matter with any oil filled radiator

Oil filled radiators have no exposed glowing element, but they still get hot and they still deserve respect. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for your model first; the points below are the widely shared basics, not a substitute.

  • Keep clear space around it. Position it away from bedding, curtains, clothes, paper and furniture, and never place anything on top of it.
  • Never cover it or dry clothes on it. Covering a heater traps heat, defeats the thermostat and is a recognised fire risk — this applies even though the surface feels less fierce than a fan heater's outlet.
  • Plug directly into a wall socket. Do not use an extension lead or adaptor, which may not be rated for a heater's sustained load.
  • Mind tipping and stability. Use it upright on a flat floor, where it cannot easily be knocked over, with the cable where nobody trips. Tip-over protection helps, but placement comes first.
  • Check it each season. Look for damage to the cable, plug and casing, and any sign of oil leaking. If you see either, stop using it.
  • Unattended use: only if the instructions allow it. Overnight, use the thermostat, keep the clear space, and consider a timer.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to run an oil filled radiator per hour in the UK?

At an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, the cost at full power is about 16.8p per hour for a 700W model, 24p for 1,000W, 36p for 1,500W, 48p for 2,000W and 60p for 2,500W. Thermostat cycling usually brings the real average down once the room is warm. Your own figure is kilowatts multiplied by your price per kWh.

Do oil filled radiators cost less once the thermostat starts cycling?

Yes, in practice. The full-power figure applies while the element is heating. Once the room reaches the thermostat setting, the element switches on and off to hold the temperature, so the average power over the hour is lower than the rated wattage. How much lower depends on the room, the weather and the setting, which is why no single cycling figure is honest for every home.

Does an oil filled radiator keep warming a room after it is switched off?

Yes, for a while. The oil inside holds heat and releases it gradually as the radiator cools, so warmth fades rather than stopping instantly. That residual heat is not free extra energy — it is heat you already paid for, stored briefly — but it does make the temperature fall more gently than with a fan heater.

Is an oil filled radiator good for a bedroom overnight?

It can suit a bedroom because it is near-silent, has no fan blowing air around, and gives steady, gentle heat. Only use one overnight if the manufacturer's instructions allow it, keep it clear of bedding and furniture, plug it directly into a wall socket, and never cover it or dry clothes on it. Many people find pre-warming the room, then turning it down or off, is enough.

Is an oil filled radiator cheaper to run than a fan heater?

At the same wattage and the same price per kWh, an hour at full power costs exactly the same — electricity is priced by the kilowatt-hour, not by the type of heater. The difference is behaviour: a fan heater warms air quickly and stops the moment it is off, while an oil radiator warms up slowly, cycles gently and keeps releasing stored heat after switch-off.
