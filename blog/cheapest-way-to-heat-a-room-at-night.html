Cheapest Way to Heat a Room at Night: Real Cost Per Hour Compared

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every cost below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

On a cold evening, most of us are not trying to heat a whole house. We are trying to feel warm in one place: on a sofa, at a desk, or in bed. That distinction is the whole answer to this guide. The cheapest way to heat a room at night is usually not a better heater. It is to stop paying to heat rooms, walls and hallways that nobody is using — and to warm the person first, the room second.

Below we compare the options most homes actually have: an electric blanket, a heated throw, a plug-in space heater, an oil-filled radiator, and turning on central heating for one radiator. We will not give you a single magic price, because there is not one. Electricity and gas prices differ between the UK, the US and Canada, between providers in the same city, and even between tariffs in the same house. What does not change is the maths.

The only formula you need

For any electric heater, the running cost works like this:

Cost per hour = power in kilowatts × your electricity price per kWh
A 2 kW heater is 2 units of electricity per hour at full power. A 100 watt blanket is 0.1 units per hour. Multiply either figure by whatever you actually pay per kWh — it is on your bill, or on your regulator's price-cap or utility page — and you have your real cost. Everything else in this guide is just that formula applied honestly.

Two details matter. First, a thermostat changes the average, not the maximum: a 2 kW oil-filled radiator that cycles on and off might average well under 2 kW once a room is warm. Second, gas is usually priced per kWh (in the UK), per therm (in much of the US) or per cubic metre (in Canada), and a boiler also loses some heat in converting fuel to warm water. So a gas comparison needs one extra step, which we cover below.

1. Warm the person: electric blankets and heated throws

This is why electric blankets appear in almost every honest discussion of night-time heating. A blanket drawing around a tenth of a kilowatt uses, in an hour, roughly the electricity a 2 kW heater uses in about three minutes. Used for a few hours before and during sleep, instead of heating a bedroom all evening, the saving over a winter can be substantial — how substantial depends entirely on your tariff and how you used to heat the room, which is why we do not print a single savings figure here.

There are limits. A blanket warms a bed, not a bathroom trip. It does nothing for damp or condensation in a cold room. And it must be used properly: follow the manufacturer's instructions, never use a damaged or very old blanket, do not fold it while it is on, keep controls and cables in good condition, and follow local guidance on who should and should not use one — manufacturers and fire services publish this, and it matters more than any saving.

A heated throw is the same idea for the evening: you, on a sofa, warm within minutes, while the room itself can stay a degree or two cooler. For many households, “throw in the evening, blanket at night” is the lowest-cost comfortable pattern there is.

2. Warm one room: plug-in heaters

If a room genuinely needs heating — a home office, a child's room before bed, a room with a damp problem — a plug-in heater heats only that space, which is a real advantage over firing up a whole system.

A fan or ceramic heater warms air quickly and is useful for short bursts, but at full power it is among the most expensive ways to run heat hour after hour: it is delivering up to around 2 kW, and you pay for every kilowatt-hour of it. An oil-filled radiator warms more slowly, keeps giving off heat after it switches off, and its thermostat means its average draw over an evening is usually lower than its maximum rating suggests. Neither is “cheap” or “expensive” in itself — the bill is power × time × your price per kWh, and time is the part people underestimate. A heater left on “for a bit” for five hours costs five times the one-hour figure.

Safety is non-negotiable here: keep heaters clear of bedding, curtains and furniture, never use them to dry clothes, never leave them running unattended or overnight unless the manufacturer explicitly designs and approves the model for that use, and plug them directly into a wall socket rather than an extension lead, as most manufacturers and fire services advise.

3. Warm the system: central heating for one room

It feels wasteful to run a boiler for one bedroom, and sometimes it is — but not always. On many standard tariffs, gas delivers a unit of heat for less than electricity does, so a well-run system heating a few rooms can cost less per room than a plug-in electric heater in each. The catch is that most systems do not truly heat one room: the boiler fires, pipes lose heat on the way, and rooms without thermostatic radiator valves warm up whether you want them to or not.

If you have thermostatic radiator valves, turning down radiators in unused rooms and keeping doors closed makes “one warm room” much closer to reality. If you do not, compare honestly: your realistic choice may be “the whole floor, on gas” versus “one room, on electricity” — and the formula, with your two unit prices, will tell you which wins in your house. Boilers are also most efficient running at steady, moderate flow temperatures; repeatedly blasting a system for twenty minutes is rarely the cheapest pattern, whatever the fuel.

Country notes: UK, US and Canada

United Kingdom

Many households are on tariffs linked to the Ofgem price cap, expressed in pence per kWh for both gas and electricity, with electricity typically costing several times more per kWh than gas. That gap is why the blanket-versus-radiator comparison is so stark in the UK, and why heating one person electrically can beat heating a room with gas while heating a whole room electrically usually cannot. Always use the unit rates on your own bill — capped and fixed tariffs differ. Households struggling with bills can also check what support their supplier and local council currently offer, as schemes change.

United States

Prices and heating systems vary far more by state: gas furnaces, electric baseboards, heat pumps and oil heat all change the maths. A heat pump, where fitted, usually delivers heat much more cheaply per hour than a resistive space heater, because it moves heat rather than making it — so “turn the heat pump down and use a blanket” and “turn the heat pump off and use a space heater” can point in opposite directions. Check your utility bill for your per-kWh and per-therm rates, and be especially careful with space-heater safety guidance from your local fire department.

Canada

Long, colder winters and province-by-province pricing (including time-of-use electricity pricing in some provinces) make timing part of the saving: where off-peak rates exist, pre-warming a bed with an electric blanket during cheaper hours can cost noticeably less than the same heat at peak time. In much of Canada the bigger risk is not comfort but the house itself — rooms should not be left so cold that pipes are at risk of freezing. Local utility and provincial guidance on minimum safe indoor temperatures is worth following over any cost tip, including ours.

Before you buy anything: the free fixes

  • Stop the draughts first. A draft excluder, closed curtains at dusk and a rug on a cold floor keep heat you have already paid for. No heater comparison beats heat that does not escape.
  • Close doors and heat fewer rooms. Every option above gets cheaper when the warm air has less space to fill.
  • Lower, longer, steadier. For most systems, a slightly lower temperature for the hours you are actually home beats short, hot blasts.
  • Check your real unit price today. One minute with your latest bill turns every general guide — including this one — into your exact answer.

So, what is cheapest?

For a person sitting or sleeping in one place, an electric blanket or heated throw is cheapest per hour by a wide margin, because it uses roughly a tenth of the power of a room heater. For a room that must be warm, compare your gas and electricity unit prices: a thermostat-controlled radiator or oil-filled heater, in a draught-proofed room with the door closed, will almost always beat a fan heater left running at full power. And for the whole house on a genuinely cold night, a properly run central system is usually the sensible choice — comfort and protecting the building matter too.

The honest answer was never a single product. It is a habit: warm the person first, the room second, the house last — and do the one-minute calculation with your own tariff before spending anything.
