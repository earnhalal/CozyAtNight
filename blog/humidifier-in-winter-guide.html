Do You Need a Humidifier in Winter? Benefits, Drawbacks and Running Costs

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. This article is general information about indoor comfort, not medical advice — if dry air symptoms persist or worry you, talk to a clinician.

A humidifier in winter is one of those purchases people make at 2 a.m., throat scratchy, lips cracked, clicking buy before they have asked the obvious question: is my air actually too dry? Sometimes the answer is genuinely yes. Sometimes the real problem is a bedroom that is too warm, too cold, or simply a home that already has too much moisture, not too little — in which case adding more makes things worse, not better.

This guide works through it in order: why indoor air dries out, the general signs it may be too dry, how to measure before you spend anything, the real benefits and drawbacks, and what a small humidifier costs to run and keep clean.

Why indoor air gets so dry in winter

It starts outside. Cold air simply holds less moisture than warm air, so winter air arrives dry to begin with. Then we heat it. When that cold, dry air is warmed indoors, its relative humidity falls further — the air could hold more moisture at its new temperature, but no new moisture has been added, so it feels drier still. The longer the heating runs, the drier the indoor air tends to become, which is why the driest rooms are often the warmest ones.

That is why dry-air problems are worst in very cold spells and in homes heated hard all day. It connects to the wider picture in our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night: how you heat a bedroom changes not just its temperature, but how its air feels to breathe.

Signs your indoor air may be too dry

None of the following proves anything on its own — these are general signs people commonly notice in dry homes, not a diagnosis, and each can have other causes. Taken together, though, they are a reasonable prompt to measure.

  • Dry skin and lips that feel tight or crack despite your usual routine.
  • A scratchy throat or dry nose on waking, easing during the day.
  • Static shocks from carpets, clothing, door handles and each other.
  • Cracking or shrinking wood — floorboards, furniture joints and musical instruments reacting to dry air.
  • Houseplants struggling at the leaf edges despite sensible watering.

If none of that sounds familiar, your humidity may already be fine — and the next section matters even more.

Measure first: what should indoor humidity be in winter?

Before buying anything, measure. A simple hygrometer — a small device that reads relative humidity — tells you where your home actually sits, room by room, for very little compared with any appliance. Feelings mislead: a cold room can feel damp at a humidity level a warm room would feel comfortable at.

The commonly cited comfortable indoor range is about 30–50% relative humidity. In very cold weather, staying nearer the lower end of that range reduces condensation on cold windows and walls, because warm moist air meeting a cold surface is exactly how that water appears. If your reading is already inside the range, a humidifier is unlikely to change how you feel. If it is consistently below it, you have a real case. If it is above it, skip ahead to the drawbacks — your home's problem is the opposite one.

A humidifier in winter: the genuine benefits

When air is genuinely too dry, bringing it back into the comfortable range can help in a few modest, honest ways. Skin and lips may feel less tight. Throats and noses that felt scratchy on waking may feel easier, which can make the bedroom more comfortable for sleep — dry air is one of several winter sleep disruptors we cover in why you cannot sleep in winter and what actually helps. Wooden furniture, floors and instruments also prefer air that is not extremely dry, and can crack or shrink less in a home kept nearer the middle of the range.

Notice the honest framing: these are comfort benefits, not cures. A humidifier does not treat colds, allergies, snoring or any medical condition, and this guide makes no such claim. It changes one measurable property of your air — for some households, enough to matter every night.

The drawbacks: too much humidity, and dirty machines

Humidity has a ceiling as well as a floor. Push indoor air too moist for too long and the excess water goes somewhere: it condenses on cold windows, walls and corners, and surfaces that stay damp can grow mould. Sustained high humidity also suits dust mites, which thrive in warm, moist bedding and carpets. In a home that already fights damp, a humidifier is usually the wrong purchase entirely.

The second drawback is the machine itself. A humidifier is a tank of standing water with warm, wet parts — an inviting place for mould and bacteria if it is neglected. A dirty humidifier can spread what grows in it into the air it is meant to improve. Cleaning is not an optional extra; it is part of owning one. If you know you will not keep up with it, that is a fair reason not to buy one.

What does a humidifier cost to run?

Less than most heating, by a long way. Small room humidifiers typically use roughly 25–50W — check the label on any specific model — the same low bracket as heated bedding rather than heaters. Using our usual formula, cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh: a 40W unit is 0.04 kW, so at the clearly illustrative rates we use across this site — 16 US cents per kWh, or 24p per kWh in the UK — it costs about 0.6 cents, or just under 1p, per hour. Run overnight, that is roughly 5 cents or 8p. The real ongoing cost is rarely the electricity; it is your time cleaning it, plus any filters, wicks or distilled water its design calls for.

Cleaning: the routine that is not optional

As a general routine: empty and rinse the tank daily or near-daily rather than topping up old water, and descale or disinfect it weekly, following your manufacturer's instructions, which take priority over any general guide. Let parts dry when the machine is out of use, and never let water sit in a stored humidifier. If you see visible growth or smell a musty odour, follow the manual. This small weekly discipline is the difference between a humidifier that helps a room and one that works against it.

No-humidifier alternatives worth trying first

A few low-effort steps can nudge bedroom air without any appliance. Lowering bedroom heat slightly can make air feel less parched — and pairs well with the guidance in our article on the best bedroom temperature for sleep in winter, since most people sleep better slightly cooler anyway. Drying laundry indoors releases moisture: that can genuinely help a very dry home, but it worsens a damp one, so only use it if your hygrometer says you have room for more moisture. Houseplants add a little humidity, but their effect is minor. A bowl of water near a radiator is the traditional trick — mild at best, harmless in a dry room, and worth trying for a few nights while your hygrometer tells you whether it moves the number.

Frequently asked questions

What should indoor humidity be in winter?

A commonly cited comfortable indoor range is about 30–50% relative humidity. In very cold weather, staying nearer the lower end of that range reduces condensation on cold windows and walls. The only way to know where your home sits is to measure it with a hygrometer rather than guessing from how the air feels.

Can a humidifier help you sleep?

It can help some people, indirectly. If dry air is leaving your throat or nose feeling scratchy and you are waking uncomfortable, raising very low humidity into the comfortable range may make the bedroom feel easier to sleep in. A humidifier is not a treatment for snoring, sleep apnoea or any other sleep problem, and if poor sleep continues, it deserves proper attention rather than another gadget.

Can a humidifier cause mould?

A humidifier itself does not cause mould, but too much humidity can. If indoor humidity stays too high for too long, moisture condenses on cold windows, walls and other surfaces, and mould can grow where surfaces stay damp. A dirty humidifier can also spread what grows inside its tank into the air. Measuring humidity, avoiding over-humidifying and cleaning the unit as instructed are what keep this risk down.

How often should you clean a humidifier?

As a general routine, empty and rinse the tank daily or near-daily, and descale or disinfect it weekly, following your manufacturer's instructions, which take priority over any general guide. Cleaning is not optional with a humidifier: a neglected tank can grow mould and bacteria and then spread them into the room the machine is meant to make more comfortable.

Is a humidifier worth it in winter?

It can be, if your indoor air genuinely measures very dry and you notice the signs — dry skin, a scratchy throat on waking, static shocks or cracking wood. It is less worthwhile if your home already sits in the comfortable range, or if your home struggles with damp and condensation, where adding moisture makes things worse. Measure first, and only then decide.
