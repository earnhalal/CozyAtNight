Does Window Insulation Film Work? An Honest Look at Shrink-Film Kits

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Film must never be fitted over a window you rely on for escape, and vents must never be covered.

Ask does window insulation film work and the honest answer is: yes, within clear limits. Shrink-film kits — a sheet of clear film taped around the window frame and tightened drum-flat with warm air from a hairdryer — work by trapping a still layer of air between the film and the glass. Still air is a genuine insulator: it is the same principle as the gap inside a double-glazed unit. On single-pane windows, which lose heat faster than almost any other part of a home, that trapped layer noticeably cuts heat loss through the glass and seals the frame's draughts at the same time. What film cannot do is fix a rotten frame, insulate a wall, or match a proper secondary-glazed unit.

This guide explains where film earns its few dollars, where it is pointless, and how to fit it so it survives the winter — a natural companion to the draught work in our draught fixes under $25 and the window-sealing in how to draught proof sash windows yourself.

What the film actually does: two jobs in one

Job one is insulation. A single pane of glass conducts heat quickly; room air warms the glass, the glass warms the outdoors. Adding the film's air pocket slows that path — the air layer does the insulating, the film just holds it still. The effect is largest exactly where single glazing is worst: big old panes, steel-framed windows, and the glass doors and sidelights many homes forget are single-glazed too.

Job two is draught-proofing, and for many homes it matters more. The film is taped to the frame or surrounding trim, sealing every crack between sash and frame in one sheet — the leaks you'd otherwise chase one by one with tape and strips. If your window's problem is moving air more than cold glass, film fixes it in an afternoon. Our guide to how to find draughts in your home will tell you which problem you have: tissue fluttering at the edges means draughts; a cold sheet of glass with no flutter means heat loss through the pane.

Where film works best — and where it does not

Best case: single-pane windows in decent frames, in rooms you heat, on windows you do not need to open until spring. Renters love it for a reason: it goes up in autumn, comes down clean in spring, and takes the paint question off the table when fitted to the trim as the kit directs. It also earns its keep on the secondary windows nobody wants to spend real money on — the landing, the spare room, the bathroom (though bathroom moisture shortens its life).

Weak cases: modern double glazing, where the unit already traps its air layer and film adds very little; windows with failed, misted sealed units, where the problem is inside the unit; and any window whose frame is rotten or whose leaks are between frame and wall — that gap needs sealing, not covering. Film also cannot help a window you must open daily for ventilation: it seals the window shut for the season, so choose which windows get it, and never fit it over a window designated as a fire escape route.

Fitting it so it lasts the winter

Most failures are fitting failures. Clean and dry the frame or trim first — tape on dust or damp paint lets go by December. Apply the double-sided tape in one continuous line with no gaps, cut the film generously oversize, press it on from the top down, then shrink it with a hairdryer on a warm setting, working from the centre outwards until wrinkles vanish and the film is taut and clear. Trim the excess. A taut sheet resists the knocks and condensation cycles that loosen a baggy one. Expect to redo bathroom and kitchen windows more often — steam is tape's enemy — and keep spare film for mid-winter repairs; a torn corner can be patched with clear tape rather than starting again.

Condensation, and the honest verdict

Film changes where condensation forms: moisture that would have condensed on the cold glass may now appear on the film or, if the seal leaks, between film and glass. Wipe and ventilate as usual — the morning routine in our guide to stopping condensation on bedroom windows applies unchanged — and treat persistent water trapped behind the film as a sign to reseal or refit that window.

Verdict: for single-pane windows you can leave shut, film is one of the best value-for-money winter jobs there is — a real insulating layer and a complete draught seal for the price of a coffee or two per window. For modern double glazing it is mostly pointless; for failed units and rotten frames it is a sticking plaster over a problem that needs a tradesperson. Know which window you have, and film will not disappoint you.

Does window insulation film work on sash windows, French doors and rented homes?

On sash windows it is doubly effective, because it seals the meeting-rail and side gaps that make sashes draughty — the same leaks our guide to how to draught proof sash windows yourself fixes permanently with brush pile. Film is the seasonal, reversible version of that job. On French doors and glazed doors it works too, fitted to the frame, though foot traffic and handles make knocks and tears more likely; fit it after the autumn deep-clean and keep patch tape handy.

For renters it is close to ideal: no screws, no paint damage when the tape is warmed and peeled slowly at removal, and a visible improvement the first cold night. Check your tenancy before fitting anything adhesive, as with any change, and keep one window per room film-free if you depend on windows for fresh air — a home still needs deliberate ventilation, and bedrooms in particular should not be sealed airtight against the night. Landlords, for their part, rarely object to a tenant removing a winter measure without trace; when in doubt, a quick message first costs nothing.

Cost is part of the verdict: a kit typically covers several windows for less than a single meal out, which is why film survives every winter-cost roundup despite being, essentially, plastic and tape. When a measure this cheap produces a feel-it-the-same-night difference on the worst glass in the house, the honest answer to the title question stays yes.

Frequently asked questions

Does window insulation film work on single-pane windows?

Yes — that is its best case. The film traps a still layer of air against the glass, and still air is a real insulator, so heat loss through the pane drops noticeably. It also seals the frame's draughts in the same step, which for many homes matters just as much.

Is window film worth it on double glazing?

Usually not. A double-glazed unit already contains its insulating air or gas layer, so film adds very little. If a double-glazed window is cold or draughty, the cause is more likely failed seals, a misted unit or frame leaks — problems film cannot fix.

Can I still open a window with insulation film on it?

No. The film seals the window for the season, so only fit it to windows you can leave shut until spring. Never fit film over a window you rely on for ventilation you cannot get elsewhere, and never over a designated fire escape window.

How long does window insulation film last?

Fitted well to a clean, dry frame, a sheet normally lasts the full winter season and is removed in spring. Steam-heavy rooms like bathrooms and kitchens shorten its life — the tape is the weak point — and small tears can be patched with clear tape rather than refitting.

Does window film stop condensation?

It moves it rather than stopping it: moisture may condense on the film instead of the glass, or behind the film if the seal leaks. Keep up the normal wipe-and-ventilate routine, and treat water persistently trapped behind the film as a sign to reseal or refit that window.
