Oil Filled Radiator vs Fan Heater: Which Is Cheaper to Run?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Electricity prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

If you are comparing oil filled radiator vs fan heater which is cheaper to run, the honest short answer will disappoint anyone hoping for a simple winner: at the same wattage, both cost exactly the same per hour at full power. A 2kW oil filled radiator and a 2kW fan heater use the same electricity, for the same reason. Where they genuinely differ is how they deliver heat, how long each job takes, and how much total energy you use before you switch off. That difference — not the hourly rate — is what this guide explains.

Below are the formula, worked UK and US examples, and how to match each heater to the job. For the oil radiator figures alone, see how much it costs to run an oil filled radiator per hour.

Oil filled radiator vs fan heater which is cheaper to run? The formula first

Every electric heater is billed the same way: cost per hour = kilowatts × price per kWh. Divide the wattage by 1,000 to get kilowatts. A 1,000W heater is 1kW, a 2,000W heater is 2kW. Multiply by the price per kWh on your bill and you have the cost for each hour at full power. The heater's shape does not enter the calculation, because all plug-in electric heaters convert electricity into heat at effectively the same rate.

Both are illustrative only — your own tariff may be higher or lower, and time-of-use plans change by the hour. Find the per-kWh figure on your bill and use the same formula; the method never changes.

That table settles the headline question: pick any row and both heaters cost the same. What changes your bill is the wattage you choose, the rate you pay, and — crucially — how many kilowatt-hours the whole job takes. Claims that one style uses dramatically less electricity at the same wattage should be treated with suspicion.

Why the total for the job can still differ

A fan heater gives instant, directional heat: switch it on and warm air is blowing towards you within seconds. That suits short jobs — taking the chill off a bathroom, warming your feet under a desk, or a quick boost while you dress. Because you feel warm almost immediately, you switch off sooner. For a 15 to 30 minute burst, that responsiveness is the whole point, and total energy used can be small even though the heater is powerful.

An oil filled radiator works the opposite way. Its element first heats oil sealed inside the body, and the warm metal then radiates heat into the room. That takes time — see how long an oil filled radiator takes to heat a room. The reward is steadier heat: once hot, the room warms evenly, without a stream of air aimed at one spot, and the heater keeps radiating after it switches off.

That afterglow is often described as free heat. It is not. It is stored heat you already paid for while the oil was warming up, simply released later. The delay is still useful — it smooths the swings between hot blasts and cold gaps — but it does not change the cost per hour, and it makes a short session inefficient: if you only need twenty minutes, much of what you paid for is still sitting in the oil when you leave.

Duty cycle: why an evening often ends up closer than you expect

Full power is only the starting point. A heater with a thermostat works hard to reach its setting, then cycles on and off to hold it. That on-off pattern is the duty cycle: a heater on for half of each hour averages half its full-power cost, whichever style it is.

Here is what marketing rarely mentions: over a whole evening, the room itself decides most of the energy. Heat escapes constantly through walls, windows, floors and draughts, and any heater must replace exactly that lost heat to hold the temperature. So a thermostat-cycled oil radiator and a fan heater of similar size, holding the same temperature in the same room, often end up using surprisingly similar total energy. The oil model releases heat in waves; the fan model tops up in shorter bursts. The pattern feels different, but the room's heat loss is the real bill.

You also pay extra whenever you overheat, heat an empty room, or warm a space long before you need it. Closing doors, fixing obvious draughts and only heating the room you are in save more than swapping one plug-in style for another. Three common jobs, matched honestly

Instead of asking which heater is cheaper in the abstract, ask which one fits the job in front of you. The table below sums up the three situations readers ask about most.

The bathroom row deserves its caveat: moisture and small spaces shrink your safety margins, so a manufacturer's bathroom rating and placement instructions come before any preference for speed. If a fan heater is not rated for that room, do not use it there.

For the bedroom, if you want air moved a little more, see convector heater vs oil filled radiator for a bedroom. Heating the bed rather than the whole room is often the cheapest night-time move of all.

Comfort counts too. Fan heaters can feel drying and noisy, and the warmth vanishes when they stop, which tempts people to run them longer. Oil filled radiators are usually silent and feel more like a traditional radiator, but are heavier and slower to respond. Neither trait changes the hourly formula — they change how long you run the heater, and that is where money is saved or wasted.

Safety rules that apply to both

Keep generous clearances around either heater — well away from bedding, curtains, sofas and anything that could fall onto it. Never cover a heater or drape clothes over it, and keep it on a flat, stable floor where it cannot be tipped.

Plug it directly into a wall socket, never an extension lead or multi-way adaptor, which are not built for a heavy load sustained for hours. Choose a model with tip-over and overheat protection, and follow the manufacturer's instructions over any general guide. Our guide on how to choose a space heater covers sizing and safety in depth. Never leave a portable heater unattended or running while you sleep unless the instructions for your exact model explicitly allow it.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper to run per hour, an oil filled radiator or a fan heater?

Neither, if they are rated at the same wattage. A 2kW oil filled radiator and a 2kW fan heater both cost 48p per hour at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, or 32 cents at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh. Electricity is billed by the kilowatt-hour, so the wattage and your rate decide the hourly cost, not the heater style.

Why does an oil filled radiator still feel warm after it is switched off?

Because the oil and metal hold heat that was stored while the heater was running. That warmth is released slowly after switch-off, which can make a room feel comfortable for a little longer. It is stored heat you have already paid for, not free energy, and it does not make the radiator cheaper per hour at the same wattage.

Which is better for a quick warm-up?

A fan heater is usually better for a quick warm-up of 15 to 30 minutes. It blows warm air straight away and you can feel it within moments, so it suits a cold bathroom in the morning or taking the chill off a room you will only use briefly. An oil filled radiator needs time to heat its oil first, so much of a short session is spent warming the heater itself.

Which is better for heating a room for several hours?

An oil filled radiator often suits several steady hours better. Once warm, it gives even, quiet heat and cycles on and off on its thermostat, and it keeps releasing stored heat during the off periods. Over a whole evening the total energy used is mostly decided by how much heat the room loses, so the two heater types can end up closer in cost than many people expect.

Can I leave an oil filled radiator or fan heater running overnight?

Only follow the manufacturer's instructions for the exact model you own, and never leave a portable heater running unattended unless those instructions explicitly allow that use. Keep either type clear of bedding and curtains, plug it directly into a wall socket, never cover it, and choose a model with tip-over and overheat protection. For most bedrooms, warming the bed itself is a safer and cheaper overnight option.
