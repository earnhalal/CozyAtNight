How to Clean a Humidifier Properly: The Daily, Weekly and Descale Routine

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only — always unplug a humidifier before cleaning it, and follow the manual for your own model wherever the two differ.

Knowing how to clean a humidifier properly is the difference between a machine that quietly makes winter air comfortable and one that quietly grows slime in its own tank. A humidifier is, at heart, a tank of standing water with a fan or a vibrating plate attached, and standing water anywhere in a house rewards neglect. The good news is that the routine is simple and cheap: a little attention every day, one proper clean each week, and a descale whenever mineral crust starts to build.

Skip it, and the signs arrive in a predictable order — a musty smell when the machine starts, a slippery film inside the tank, white crust around the base, and, on misting models, tank contents carried into the room, which is part of the story behind why a humidifier leaves white dust. None of it needs special products. It needs a schedule.

Safety first: the rules that never change

Unplug the machine before you clean it. Every time, without exception. Take the plug out of the socket before you open anything, empty anything or refill anything. Cleaning means putting water exactly where the electrics live, and an unplugged machine cannot be switched on accidentally mid-clean, cannot run dry while dismantled, and keeps wet hands away from a live appliance. It takes two seconds and it removes an entire category of risk.

Three more rules belong with it. First, never mix cleaning products — vinegar is an acid, and acids mixed with bleach produce dangerous gases; use one product at a time and rinse thoroughly between products. Second, never immerse the base, motor or cord unless your manual explicitly says that part can be washed; most bases are wiped, not dunked. Third, the manual wins: designs differ, and if your maker's instructions contradict a general routine like this one, follow the maker. Work on a folded towel so small parts stay put and drips stay off the floor.

What you need: a short, cheap kit

Everything on this list is probably already in your kitchen. Plain white vinegar does the descaling and most of the cleaning. A drop of ordinary washing-up liquid handles greasy fingerprints and general grime on removable plastic parts. A soft cloth or non-scratch sponge protects the plastic from scratches that give scale somewhere to grip. A small bottle brush — or an old, soft toothbrush — reaches inside the tank neck and around the cap threads, and cotton buds handle the corners. Finally, a clean, dry towel to rest parts on while they air-dry. That is the whole kit; anything stronger belongs only where a manual specifically calls for it.

The daily two-minute routine

Daily care is what makes the weekly clean easy. When the machine stops for the day — or before you refill it — empty out the water that is left rather than topping up the same water for days on end. Yesterday's water has been sitting warm and still, and every top-up adds fresh minerals to old water. Rinse the tank with fresh water, shake it, empty it again, and wipe the base reservoir where the tank sits.

Then make a small decision: if the machine is going straight back on, refill with fresh water and carry on. If it is off until tonight or tomorrow, leave the tank cap off and the parts separated on a towel so everything air-dries. Drying is a cleaning step in its own right — the films that grow in tanks grow in damp, not in dry air. On evaporative models, treat the wick as the manual directs, since some wicks are meant to stay damp between uses; the tank and reservoir advice still applies.

The weekly deep clean, step by step

Once a week while the machine is in regular use, give it the full clean. The steps are the same for almost every design:

  1. Unplug and empty. Plug out, tank off, every drop of old water poured away.
  2. Dismantle what the manual allows. Tank, cap, water tray and any removable parts come apart. If a part is not meant to come off, leave it.
  3. Soak with vinegar. Fill the tank with plain white vinegar — or a half-and-half vinegar and water mix for a light week — and pour vinegar into the base reservoir too, enough to cover the scaled areas. Leave it for about 30 minutes to an hour.
  4. Brush gently. Tip the vinegar out into the sink, then brush the softened scale and film away with the bottle brush or soft toothbrush. Corners, cap threads and the outlet where mist leaves deserve the most attention.
  5. Wash the other removable parts in warm water with a drop of washing-up liquid, and rinse them.
  6. Rinse everything until the vinegar smell is gone. Leftover vinegar in a running machine gives you a room that smells faintly of a chip shop. Rinse twice if in doubt.
  7. Air-dry fully on the towel, reassemble, and refill with fresh water.

One honest note about disinfecting: some manuals include an optional disinfection step with a specific product and dilution. If yours does, and you choose to do it, follow its dilution and rinsing instructions exactly, and do it as a separate operation from the vinegar clean — never in the same water, never straight after without thorough rinsing between. If your manual does not mention it, the vinegar routine plus daily drying is the routine; do not improvise stronger chemistry.

Descaling: how to tell it is due, and what it does

Descaling is the vinegar soak aimed at one target: mineral crust. You will know it is due when you can see or feel it — a white or chalky coating inside the tank, around the base reservoir, or on the vibrating plate of an ultrasonic model, sometimes with the machine's output visibly fading as the plate fur gets thicker. How fast it builds depends entirely on your water. If your kettle furs up in a fortnight, your humidifier is on a similar clock; in a soft-water area, a descale every few weeks may be plenty.

Understand what descaling cannot do: it removes the minerals already in the machine, but it does nothing about the minerals in tomorrow's tap water. That is why it pairs with — rather than replaces — the weekly clean, and why the white-dust guide linked above discusses changing the water itself if crust and dust keep coming back.

Evaporative models: the wick needs its own routine

If your machine blows air across a wick, the wick is a consumable, not a permanent part. Rinse it only if the manual allows, and never scrub it with soap or vinegar unless the maker says so — wick material damages easily, and a damaged wick humidifies badly. Let it dry as directed between uses where the manual asks for that. When a wick stays discoloured, stiff or musty-smelling after cleaning, it is finished: replace it. No cleaning routine resurrects a spent wick, and running one is how an otherwise well-kept machine starts smelling like a damp cupboard.

Mould prevention: the habits that do the real work

Most mould problems in humidifiers are not cleaning failures so much as storage and standing-water failures. The habits that prevent them are unglamorous. Never leave water sitting in the machine while it is off for days — empty it, rinse it, dry it. At the end of the season, clean and descale before you put it away, and store it completely dry; a machine stored wet in spring greets you in autumn already grown. Run the machine to a comfortable humidity rather than to a fog — our guide to what indoor humidity should be in winter explains the targets — because a room kept too damp feeds problems far beyond the tank. And if a musty smell appears, stop, deep-clean, and only then run it again. If growth keeps returning in a part you cannot reach to clean, that part — or the machine — has reached the end of its honest life.

Cleaning also interacts with the type of machine you chose. Frequently asked questions

How often should you clean a humidifier properly?

As a general rhythm, give the tank a rinse and fresh water daily, a proper clean with vinegar about once a week while the machine is in regular use, and a descale whenever you see mineral crust building up — more often in hard-water areas. Your manual's schedule for your own model always wins over a general routine like this one.

Can I use vinegar to clean a humidifier?

Yes — plain white vinegar is the cleaner most manufacturers point to for tanks and bases, because it dissolves mineral scale and is easy to rinse away completely. Soak the affected parts, brush gently, then rinse until no vinegar smell remains. Check your manual first, as a few models specify a particular descaler instead.

Why must I unplug a humidifier before cleaning it?

Because cleaning means opening the machine up and putting water exactly where the electrics live. An unplugged machine cannot be switched on accidentally mid-clean, cannot run dry while dismantled, and keeps wet hands away from a live appliance. It takes two seconds and removes the whole category of risk.

Can I mix vinegar and bleach to clean a humidifier?

Never. Vinegar is an acid, and mixing acids with bleach produces dangerous gases that can seriously harm you. Use one cleaning product at a time, exactly as your manual describes, and rinse everything thoroughly between different products. If the manual does not mention bleach at all, do not improvise with it.

What should I do if I see pink slime or black spots in the tank?

Stop using the machine and give it a full deep clean and descale before its next run, following your manual. Pink films and dark spots are growth in standing water, and a misting machine sends tank contents into the room. If the growth is in a part you cannot reach or keeps returning, replace that part — or the machine — rather than running it dirty.
