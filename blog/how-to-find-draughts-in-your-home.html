How to Find Draughts in Your Home: The Candle and Incense Test

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — not a substitute for professional safety advice, especially around boilers, flues and ventilation.

Learning how to find draughts in your home is the step that comes before every fix and every purchase. A draught is simply unwanted cold air moving through a gap — under a door, around a window frame, through a keyhole — and until you know where yours are, it is easy to seal the wrong places while the real leak keeps chilling the room. This guide is methodical: three simple tests, one room-by-room checklist, and a written list that tells you what to fix first.

You need almost nothing: your hand, a sheet of tissue or lightweight paper, and — used carefully — a candle or an incense stick. Work slowly, one room at a time, and write things down as you go. Guesswork is what this process replaces.

How to find draughts in your home: start with the right conditions

Timing matters more than technique. Choose a cold, windy day if you can: wind pushes outdoor air through gaps, so a leak barely noticeable on a still day becomes an obvious cold stream, and cold air stands out clearly against warm skin, making your hand far more sensitive.

Just before you start, turn the heating off briefly. Radiators create their own gentle currents, and you do not want to mistake rising warm air for a draught, or have a radiator's warmth mask a cold leak nearby. Close internal doors as you work, so each room is judged on its own. On a mild, still day you can still check — the tissue and smoke tests below catch smaller leaks — but recheck on a windy day before committing to bigger jobs.

Test one: the back of your hand

Your hand is a surprisingly good draught detector, and the back of it is more sensitive than your palm. Hold it a short distance from the surface — near, not pressed against it — and move slowly along door and window edges, around letterboxes and keyholes, along skirting boards and across the loft hatch. Moving air feels distinctly colder than still air, and the strongest leaks announce themselves as a thin, steady chill.

The common mistake is rushing. Move slowly and pause at corners, hinges and latches, where seals fail first. When you feel a cold stream, trace it: under the door, around the frame, or through the letterbox itself? That detail goes on your list, because each source has a different fix.

Test two: tissue or lightweight paper

For gaps too small to feel reliably, let the air show itself. Hold a sheet of tissue, or a strip of lightweight paper, by one edge near a suspect gap and watch: in still air it hangs limp; in a draught it flutters or pulls towards the gap. Work along the whole edge, not one spot — seals often fail in a single short section while the rest is sound.

The tissue test has one great advantage over any flame: it is safe everywhere, including near curtains, bedding and upholstery, where a candle should never go. It is also the best test for floorboard gaps and loft hatch edges, where you can lay it flat and watch for movement.

Test three: candle or incense, done safely

A candle flame or incense smoke makes air movement visible, which is why this traditional test survives. Hold it near — not against — a frame or edge and watch: a steady waver, a flame leaning one way, or smoke streaming sideways instead of rising all show air on the move. Incense smoke is often clearest, because the thin trail bends at even a gentle leak.

The safety rules are not optional. Keep any flame well away from curtains, furnishings, bedding and anything flammable, and never leave a lit candle or incense stick unattended. Use a short, stable candle, keep it in your hand rather than balancing it on a frame, and put it out the moment you move on. Near fabrics or anywhere cluttered, skip the flame entirely and use the tissue test instead — it tells you the same thing with no risk attached.

The room-by-room checklist

Tests tell you how; this checklist tells you where. In each room, work through the same list in the same order, so nothing is skipped. In most homes, the same handful of places account for nearly every draught.

  • Doors. All four edges, not just the bottom — underneath, hinge side, latch side and top — on external doors first, then internal doors to unheated halls or garages.
  • Windows. The full perimeter of every frame, where two opening sections meet, and where the frame meets the wall. Cracked sealant and shrunken seals are the usual culprits.
  • Letterbox. A letterbox or mail slot is a hole in the front door by design. Feel for air around its edges and through the flap itself.
  • Keyholes. An uncovered keyhole in an external door is a small wind tunnel. Hold tissue beside it and watch — the result often surprises people.
  • Skirting boards. Run your hand or tissue along the join between skirting and floor, and skirting and wall, particularly on outside walls.
  • Floorboards. Gaps between boards, and where boards meet the wall, let cold air rise from the void below. Bare boards in older homes are prime suspects.
  • Loft hatch. A hatch is a door into the coldest space in the house. Check all four edges, and whether it sits flat.
  • Unused chimney. Hold tissue near the fireplace opening of a chimney you do not use; a steady pull shows warm air escaping up the shaft. Our companion guide explains how to block cold air from an unused fireplace with a chimney draught excluder.
  • Pipe and cupboard entries. Where pipes pass through walls, floors and cupboard backs, the holes are often larger than the pipe. Feel around each entry, including under sinks and behind bath panels.

Front doors deserve a second pass, combining several of these at once — edges, letterbox, keyhole and threshold. If yours is the worst offender, our guide to how to stop a draught coming through the front door works through each in turn.

Cold spots, thermometers and what each one proves

Sometimes you cannot feel moving air, but one corner is always cold: a draught too weak to feel, or a cold patch of wall or floor chilling the air beside it. An inexpensive thermometer can help — move it slowly around the room, pausing near frames, floor edges and outside-wall corners, and compare with the middle of the room.

Be clear about what it proves, though. A thermometer shows temperature, not moving air: only the hand, tissue and smoke tests show the draught itself. Use it as the backup, not the main tool, and keep it simple — a basic, general-purpose thermometer is all this needs.

Making your fix list: biggest gap first

Every check above should end up as a line on paper: room, location, what you felt or saw, and roughly how strong it seemed. Then reorder the list, biggest gap first. Draughts are not equal — one wide gap under a front door can undo a dozen tiny seals, and fixing in the wrong order is how people spend an afternoon and feel no difference.

With the list written, fixing becomes straightforward. Our guide to draught fixes under $25 covers the common answers — brush strips, foam tape, letterbox seals, keyhole covers, chimney balloons and the rest — in the same biggest-first spirit. Work down the list, then retest the worst spots on the next windy day.

The safety line: ventilation is not a draught

One warning sits above everything here: never mistake purpose-made ventilation for a draught to seal. Air bricks, trickle vents and boiler flues are there on purpose. Homes need fresh air, and fuel-burning appliances need to draw air in and push fumes out. Sealing an opening a boiler, furnace or gas fire depends on can cause carbon monoxide to build up indoors — an odourless gas that can be fatal. Keep a working carbon monoxide alarm near fuel-burning appliances, and if you cannot tell whether an opening is ventilation or a gap, leave it alone and ask a qualified heating engineer.

Ventilation also carries moisture out; a home sealed too tightly traps steam, which condenses and feeds mould. The goal is accidental holes closed and deliberate ones open — warm and dry, not warm and damp.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best day to look for draughts?

Choose a cold, windy day if you can. Wind pushes outdoor air through gaps and makes leaks much easier to feel, and cold outdoor air stands out clearly against warm skin. Turn the heating off briefly while you check, so currents you feel are draughts rather than warm air moving from radiators.

Is the candle test safe to use around the home?

It can be, with care: use a short, stable candle or an incense stick, keep it well away from curtains, furnishings and anything flammable, and never leave it unattended. Near fabrics, curtains or clutter, use the tissue test instead — it shows the same movement of air with no flame involved.

Should I seal every gap I find?

No. Seal unintentional gaps around doors, windows and floorboards, but never block purpose-made ventilation such as air bricks, trickle vents or boiler flues. Those openings exist to bring in fresh air and let fuel-burning appliances work safely. If you are unsure whether an opening is ventilation, leave it alone and ask a qualified professional.

What should I do once I have found my draughts?

Write them down, biggest gap first, and work through the list in that order rather than buying materials at random. A brush strip for the worst door and tape for the worst window will usually matter more than a dozen tiny fixes. Our guide to draught fixes under $25 walks through the common fixes in the same order.

Can a thermometer help me find cold spots?

Yes, as a backup to your hand. An inexpensive thermometer moved slowly around a room can confirm a colder patch near a frame, a floor edge or an outside wall corner. It shows temperature rather than moving air, though, so use it alongside the hand and tissue tests, which reveal the draught itself.
