How to Warm Up a Cold Bed Quickly: 7 Ways That Work Tonight

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

Learning how to warm up a cold bed quickly changes winter nights out of proportion to the effort. The misery is not really the cold room. It is the cold sheet against your back, the mattress that seems to pull heat out of you, and the ten shivering minutes before your own body finally warms the bedding. Every method below attacks that problem directly: put warmth into the bed before you get in, stop the sheet stealing your heat at first touch, and keep the warmth trapped where you sleep. The electric options cost cents, not dollars, and most steps need nothing you do not already own.

How to warm up a cold bed quickly — the fastest combination

If you only read one section, read this one. Switch an electric blanket or heated pad on 20–30 minutes before bed, duvet pulled fully over it. While it runs, fill a hot water bottle safely and place it near the foot of the bed. Change into warm pyjamas and clean socks, and keep the bedroom door closed. Get in, move the bottle to your feet, and turn the heated bedding down or off as its instructions advise. That stack is faster and cheaper than heating the room around the bed.

1. Pre-heat the bed with an electric blanket or heated pad

Pre-heating is the most effective step because it warms the two things you actually touch: the sheet and the top of the mattress. Switch it on well before you need it, not as you climb in: 20–30 minutes is a useful starting point, duvet pulled over the top so warmth builds in the bed. Follow your model's instructions, use a timer if it has one, and never switch it on while folded, bunched or hidden under piles of clothing.

Many people pre-heat on a higher setting, then switch to low or off for sleeping, because the warmed mattress and a good duvet hold heat for hours. If you are choosing a heated layer, our comparison of heated mattress pad vs electric blanket which is better explains the under-you versus over-you difference, and our electric blanket vs space heater guide shows why warming the bed beats warming the room on cost.

2. Use a hot water bottle — and do it safely

The hot water bottle is the oldest bed warmer in the house and still one of the best, especially at the foot of the bed, where cold lingers. Fill it in good time so it warms the lower sheet while the bed pre-heats. In a cover, it gives deep, local warmth at no cost beyond the hot water itself.

Safety is not a footnote here. Never fill a bottle with boiling water — use hot water as the maker directs, because boiling water can damage the bottle and raises the risk if it leaks. Before filling, check the bottle and stopper for cracks, splits and wear, and check for leaks after filling. Always use a cover so it never rests directly against bare skin, do not lie or sit heavily on it, do not overfill it, and replace it when it looks old.

3. Fix the cold-sheet problem with flannel and layers

Some of the shock of a cold bed is the sheet itself. A smooth sheet lies flat and cold against your skin and draws warmth away quickly at first contact, which is why the first minute feels worse than the tenth. Flannel or brushed cotton sheets fix the first-touch problem: their raised, fuzzy surface traps tiny pockets of air and touches your skin less completely, so they feel warmer instantly at the same room temperature. Swapping just the bottom sheet for a brushed one can change how the whole bed feels.

Layering above you works the same way. Shake the duvet out to restore its loft — a flattened duvet insulates less — and add a light blanket across the foot end where cold creeps in. If you read in bed, a heated throw over your legs warms you while the bed pre-heats; see heated throw vs electric blanket.

4. Warm your pyjamas and wear socks

Cold clothing undoes a warm bed, so lay your pyjamas — and clean, loose socks — on the made bed while it pre-heats, somewhere warm but not covering a switched-on electric blanket. Warm feet change how warm the rest of you feels. Choose loose sleep socks, not tight daytime pairs, and kick them off if you get too warm. Brushed winter pyjamas help in the same first-touch way as a flannel sheet.

5. Help the bedroom help you: door closed, draughts stopped

You do not need a warm bedroom to have a warm bed, but a draughty room steals heat from the bed's edges all night. Close the bedroom door well before bed, close curtains over cold glass, and block the door-bottom draught — a rolled towel works tonight. If heating runs in the evening, let the bedroom share that warmth rather than heating it specially later.

Do not overdo the room either: a room that is too warm can make sleep less comfortable once the bed itself is warm. Our guide to the best bedroom temperature for sleep in winter covers sensible room ranges, so you can pair a comfortably cool room with a properly warm bed.

6. Skip what does not work: heating the whole house for one bed

The most expensive instinct on a cold night is also the least effective here. Heating the whole house late, or running a powerful heater for hours, spends most of its energy on air, walls and rooms nobody is sleeping in — and the sheet can still feel cold at first touch, because warming air and warming bedding are different jobs. Room heaters commonly draw 750–1,500W or more; heated bedding draws roughly 50–150W. Warm the person and the bed directly.

7. Check the running cost — it is less than you think

Fear of the bill stops some people pre-heating at all, so here is the honest maths. The formula is always: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A 100W electric blanket or pad is 0.1 kW. At an illustrative US rate of 16¢ per kWh, that is 0.1 × 16¢ = about 1.6 cents per hour, so a 30-minute pre-heat costs under a cent. A 60W model costs about 0.96 cents per hour on the same maths. As a note for UK readers, at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh the 100W figure is 0.1 × 24p = about 2.4p per hour. Both rates are examples only — check your own bill and use your real price per kWh.

Tonight in 15 minutes: the routine, on a clock

Minute 0: Make the bed properly — flannel or brushed sheet on, duvet shaken out and pulled up. Switch the electric blanket or heated pad on to pre-heat, duvet over it, and close the bedroom door and curtains. Minute 2: Fill the hot water bottle safely — hot water, never boiling, stopper checked — put its cover on and place it at the foot of the bed under the duvet. Minute 5: Lay pyjamas and socks out at the warm end of the bed, put an extra light layer ready across the foot, and block the door-bottom draught with a rolled towel.

Minute 10: Warm yourself, not just the bed — a warm drink or changing out of cold daytime clothes helps. Minute 15: Bedtime. Slide the bottle to your feet, get in while the sheet is warm, and turn the heated bedding down or off as instructed. Because everything started warm, the bed usually stays comfortable for hours.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to warm up a cold bed?

Pre-heat the bed itself with an electric blanket or heated mattress pad, switched on 20 to 30 minutes before bed with the duvet pulled over it to trap the heat. Add a safely filled hot water bottle at the foot of the bed, wear warm pyjamas and socks, and keep the bedroom door closed. That warms the sheet, mattress and you, instead of wasting heat on the whole room.

How long should you pre-heat a bed with an electric blanket?

For most beds, 20 to 30 minutes on a pre-heat setting is enough to take the chill off the sheet and mattress, though very cold rooms can need a little longer. Follow your model's instructions, use any timer it provides, then turn it down or off when you get in if that is what the instructions recommend. Never leave heated bedding folded or bunched while it is on.

Is it safe to put a hot water bottle in bed?

Yes, if you follow basic rules every time. Never fill a hot water bottle with boiling water. Check the bottle and stopper for cracks, splits or wear before filling, use a cover so it does not sit directly against skin, and do not lie or sit heavily on it. Replace a bottle that looks old or damaged, and follow the maker's guidance on its lifespan.

Why do flannel sheets feel warmer than smooth cotton sheets?

Flannel and brushed cotton have a raised, fuzzy surface that holds tiny pockets of air and makes less flat contact with your skin at first touch, so they do not pull warmth away as sharply as a smooth, cold sheet. They do not create heat, but they remove the cold-sheet shock that makes a winter bed feel unwelcoming for the first few minutes.

How much does it cost to pre-heat a bed with an electric blanket?

Very little. Cost per hour is kilowatts multiplied by your price per kWh. A 100W blanket is 0.1 kW, so at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh it costs about 1.6 cents per hour — a 30-minute pre-heat is under a cent. At an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, the same blanket costs about 2.4p per hour. Check your own bill for your real rate.
