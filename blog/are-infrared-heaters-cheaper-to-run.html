Are Infrared Heaters Cheaper to Run? The Wattage Truth

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Electricity prices vary widely by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

Are infrared heaters cheaper to run than other electric heaters? At the same wattage, no. A 1kW infrared panel and a 1kW ceramic heater cost exactly the same per hour, because your bill is based on the kilowatt-hours you use, not on the shape of the heater or the language on its box. Infrared can still work out cheaper overall, but only when its delivery lets you use fewer kilowatt-hours in total.

This guide starts with that wattage truth, then looks at infrared's one real advantage, where it disappears, and the placement rules that keep one safe. It pairs with our comparison of the cheapest way to heat a room at night.

Are infrared heaters cheaper to run? The wattage truth first

Every electric heater is billed the same way: cost per hour = kilowatts × price per kWh. Divide watts by 1,000 to get kilowatts, then multiply by the rate on your bill. A 1kW heater is 1.0 kW, so at an illustrative US rate of 16¢/kWh it costs 1.0 × 16¢ = 16¢ per hour. At an illustrative UK rate of 24p/kWh, the same heater costs 1.0 × 24p = 24p per hour. The type of heater does not change that maths.

Read the first two rows together, because they are the whole argument. The infrared panel is not cheaper per hour. The 500W row is cheaper only because it is smaller — any 500W heater would show the same figure. Whenever a comparison makes infrared look dramatically cheaper, check the wattages first. Often a smaller heater is doing quiet work behind a bold headline.

That is also how to unpack claims that a heater is “90% more efficient” or will slash bills by a dramatic share. At the point of use, all electric heaters convert electricity to heat at effectively the same rate: the electricity that enters becomes heat in the room. There is no spare 90% waiting to be unlocked. What can differ is where the heat goes, how quickly you feel it, and how much of it warms empty air. Those are differences in delivery, not in the price of a kilowatt-hour.

Heating people and surfaces, not all the air

A fan heater, ceramic heater or oil filled radiator mostly works by warming air, which then circulates around the room. Infrared radiates heat outward, much as sunlight does, and that heat lands on whatever is in front of it: your skin and clothes, the sofa, the desk, the floor and the wall opposite. Those surfaces warm up and give off some gentle warmth in turn, but the air itself is not the main carrier.

This is why infrared can feel fast. Sit in its line of sight and you may feel warmth on your face and hands almost straight away, even while the room air still feels cool. A thermostat reading can mislead: the air may stay modest while the person in the beam feels perfectly comfortable. For spot heating, comfort is about the person, not the average temperature of every cubic metre of air.

When infrared can use fewer total kilowatt-hours

If the hourly cost is the same, where can a saving honestly come from? From the total: fewer kilowatts, or the same kilowatts for fewer hours. Infrared can help on both counts, but only in particular patterns of use.

A lower wattage for one spot. If you sit in much the same place all evening — a desk where you work, a sofa where you read, a workbench — a modest infrared heater aimed at that spot may keep you comfortable where warming the whole room would need a larger heater. You are heating a person and a small patch of room, not the corners and the ceiling void. That smaller target is the saving.

Less time at full use. Because the warmth is felt quickly in the beam, infrared suits intermittent use: short bursts when you get in, an hour at a desk, taking the chill off while you dress. A heater that must bring a whole room around invites you to switch it on earlier and leave it on longer. One switched on when you sit down, and off when you get up, uses fewer hours.

Feeling warm at a lower air temperature. If the beam keeps you comfortable, you may let the room air sit cooler than you would otherwise set it, and keep the main heating lower too. High ceilings strengthen the effect: warm air drifts up where nobody sits, while infrared aimed at the seats need not fill the whole volume from the ceiling down.

Where infrared does not win

The same line-of-sight logic sets the limits. Infrared does not travel around corners, through furniture or into the next room. Whatever is not facing the heater — a chair with its back to it, a spot behind a sofa, a desk tucked behind shelving — gets little direct warmth. Move out of the beam and the benefit goes with the beam, not with you.

For warming a whole room evenly, especially for several people over a long evening, a heater that warms the air is usually the simpler fit. Film nights and guests in every seat are whole-room jobs. Run an infrared heater for whole-room hours and you pay for a delivery style designed for a spot — and if you add a second unit or a much larger one to compensate, any hoped-for advantage is spent. Sizing matters whichever type you choose: our guide to what size space heater you need for your room walks through matching wattage to the space.

Infrared also does not win by being left on. A small panel running all day in an empty room uses the same electricity as any heater of that wattage in an empty room, and warms nobody. Timers, thermostats and switching off when you leave matter at least as much as heater type.

Placement rules and safety

Placement decides both whether an infrared heater works and whether it is used safely, so treat the manufacturer's instructions as part of the purchase. Follow the maker's mounting instructions exactly: whether the unit is wall-mounted, ceiling-mounted or free-standing, the height, angle and fixings it specifies, and the clearances it requires from walls, ceilings, floors and furniture. Mounted where the maker intends, it also has the line of sight it was designed for.

Surfaces get hot. Some infrared heaters run with very hot exposed surfaces, and even cooler-feeling panels need the clear space their instructions state. Keep bedding, curtains, clothing, papers and soft furnishings well clear, and keep the area in front of it free of anything that could be pushed or fall against it. Supervise children and pets around any hot surface, and take extra care around anyone who might touch it unawares.

Our general guide to how to choose a space heater covers the wider checklist — stable positioning, tip-over and overheat protection, and why a heater should plug directly into a wall outlet rather than an extension cord or power strip. Add one rule of its own: check the beam path before you settle. If a bookcase or the back of an armchair sits between the heater and your seat, that object, not you, will be warm all evening.

Frequently asked questions

Are infrared heaters cheaper to run than other electric heaters?

At the same wattage, no. A 1kW infrared panel and a 1kW ceramic heater both cost about 16 cents per hour at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh, or 24p per hour at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh. It can cost less overall only when its focused warmth lets you run a lower wattage, or the same wattage for less time.

Why do some infrared heaters claim to be far more efficient?

Those claims describe how warmth is delivered, not a different cost per kilowatt-hour. All electric heaters convert electricity to heat at effectively the same rate at the point of use. Infrared can feel warmer sooner on skin and surfaces in its line of sight — that is comfort, not a cheaper unit of electricity.

When does an infrared heater actually save money?

When it lets you heat a smaller target for less time. Aiming one at a desk or sofa spot, using it in short bursts, or feeling comfortable at a lower air temperature can reduce the total kilowatt-hours you buy. Run the same wattage for the same hours to warm a whole room, and there is no saving to expect.

Can an infrared heater warm a whole room evenly?

Not as evenly as a heater that warms the air. Infrared travels in its line of sight, so people and surfaces facing it warm first, while corners and areas behind furniture stay cooler. For even whole-room warmth over long periods, heating the air is usually the simpler fit.

Where should I place an infrared heater?

Place or mount it exactly as the manufacturer directs, with a clear line of sight to the spot you occupy and clear space around its hot surfaces. Keep it away from bedding, curtains and furniture, and never cover it or use it where its instructions say not to.
