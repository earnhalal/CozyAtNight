What Size Space Heater Do I Need for My Room? A Simple Watts-Per-Room Guide

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Sizing rules are starting estimates, not a substitute for a heating calculation for your home — insulation, climate and ceiling height all change the answer.

If you are asking what size space heater do I need for my room, start with the room itself, not the box on the shelf. Measure the floor, allow roughly 10 watts per square foot for an averagely insulated room, and you have a sensible first estimate: a 100 square foot room points to about 1,000 watts, a 150 square foot room to about 1,500 watts. This guide shows the table, the adjustments that matter, and why the standard US plug-in size stops where it does.

That rule is a starting estimate, not a promise. Two rooms with the same floor area can need different amounts of heat. What follows helps you adjust honestly — up for a cold, draughty room, down for a snug top-up. For types and controls, pair this guide with how to choose a space heater.

What size space heater do I need for my room? Start with watts per square foot

The common rule of thumb is about 10 watts per square foot of floor area — roughly 100 watts per square metre — for an averagely insulated room in a typical winter climate. Multiply the length of the room by the width to get the area, then multiply by ten. A room 12 feet by 10 feet is 120 square feet, so the estimate is 1,200 watts. Round to the nearest heater setting.

Notice what the rule assumes: average insulation, a standard ceiling, windows that close properly, and outside temperatures that are cold rather than extreme. Change any of those and the same floor area needs more or less heat. Treat the rule as the middle of a range, then work through the adjustments below before you size up, down, or stay put.

A starting estimate, clearly labelled. The 10 watts per square foot figure is a rule of thumb, not a measurement of your room. It does not replace a proper heat-loss calculation. Use it to narrow the choice, then let the adjustments — and a thermostat — do the fine work.

The adjustments that move you up or down a size

Ceiling height. The rule assumes a standard ceiling of around eight feet. Heat fills volume, not floor area, so a high or vaulted ceiling holds far more air to warm. If your ceiling is noticeably higher, treat the room as larger than its floor measurement suggests.

Insulation and age of the home. An older home with thin insulation or gaps around doors loses heat as fast as you add it; a well-insulated room holds it, so the same heater cycles less. If cold air moves at skirting boards and window frames, fix the draughts first — the estimate assumes the heat you pay for stays in the room.

Outside temperature. A heater that copes on a mild evening may fall short in a deep cold snap, because the gap between inside and outside drives heat loss. In a long, hard winter, size towards the top of your range; in a milder climate, the plain rule is usually plenty.

Windows. Large, single-glazed or poorly sealed windows shed heat and create downdraughts. A room with a wall of older glass needs more than the table suggests, and heavy curtains closed at dusk help the heater you already have.

Only heat source, or a top-up? This is the biggest adjustment. If the heater is the room's only heat, size for the full estimate. If central heating already takes the chill off and the heater only lifts one occupied corner in the evening, a smaller size — or a lower setting — can be enough.

Why 1,500 watts is the practical ceiling for a US plug-in heater

Shop for plug-in heaters in the US and almost everything tops out at about 1,500 watts. That is no coincidence. A typical US household circuit is rated at 15 amps, and a heater is a sustained, heavy load that runs for hours, not a brief burst. Around 1,500 watts is the practical ceiling that leaves a sensible margin on that circuit. For more on types and controls, see how to choose a space heater.

The same limit explains a rule people miss: a heater should not share its circuit with other heavy loads. Together they can trip the breaker — or stress wiring never meant to carry both at once. Give the heater its own circuit where you can, plug it directly into the wall socket, and treat a repeatedly tripping breaker as a warning to stop. If your room truly needs more than 1,500 watts, the honest answer is better insulation, a smaller heated zone, or fixed heating installed properly — not a bigger plug-in model.

Too small, too big, and why the thermostat does the saving

An undersized heater runs flat out and still feels cold. It never reaches the temperature you set, so it never rests, and you sit in a room that is permanently almost warm enough. An oversized heater has the opposite problem in a small room: it blasts the air hot in short cycles, overshoots into stuffy peaks, then switches off and lets the room sag.

The comfortable middle is a heater sized close to the estimate, working through its thermostat. Once the room reaches the setting, the thermostat cycles it off and on, so its average power over an evening is well below its maximum — and that cycling, not the headline wattage, is where the saving lives. What the right size costs to run

Sizing and cost are the same calculation from two sides: cost per hour = kilowatts × price per kWh. Divide watts by 1,000 to get kilowatts. At a labelled illustrative US rate of 16¢ per kWh, a 1,500W heater at full power costs 1.5 × 16¢ = 24¢ per hour, a 1,000W heater 16¢, and a 750W heater 12¢. Those are full-power ceilings — a thermostat cycling in a correctly sized room brings the real average below them.

Reading in the UK, the same formula applies at a labelled illustrative rate of 24p per kWh: a 1.5 kW heater costs about 36p per hour at full power. Always replace the illustrative rate with the figure on your own bill. For full worked tables, see how much does a space heater cost to run.

Safety is part of sizing, not an afterthought

Choose a model with tip-over protection that cuts power if it is knocked, and overheat protection that shuts it down if it gets too hot — treat both as essentials, not upgrades. Keep clearances exactly as the manufacturer states, on a flat, hard floor, away from bedding, curtains, furniture and anything that could fall across it. Plug it directly into a wall socket only: never an extension cord, power strip or adapter. Never leave a heater running unattended or while you sleep unless the instructions explicitly allow that use, keep children and pets supervised around it, and keep it out of bathrooms unless it is rated for bathroom use.

Frequently asked questions

What size space heater do I need for a 150 square foot room?

Using the starting estimate of about 10 watts per square foot, a 150 square foot room points to about 1,500 watts. That is the standard maximum for a US plug-in heater. If the room is draughty, has high ceilings or is your only heat source, it may still feel short on the coldest nights.

Is a 1,500W heater enough for a large room?

For an averagely insulated room up to about 150 square feet, usually yes as a main source, and for a larger room as a top-up alongside other heating. Beyond that size it will run for long periods and may not hold a comfortable temperature when it is very cold outside.

What if my room is bigger than the table covers?

Do not look for a bigger plug-in heater, because about 1,500 watts is the practical ceiling on a typical US 15-amp circuit. Heat a smaller lived-in zone instead, use the heater as a top-up to your main heating, or have fixed heating sized and installed by a qualified professional.

Does a bigger heater cost more to run?

At full power, yes, because cost follows kilowatts. In real use a correctly sized heater on a thermostat cycles off once the room is warm, so its average cost can be similar to a smaller heater running flat out. An undersized heater that never reaches the setting never gets that saving.

Can I use two small heaters instead of one large one?

Only with care, and never as a way around the circuit limit. Two heaters on the same circuit can overload it, so they would need separate circuits, each plugged directly into a wall socket. For most single rooms, one correctly sized heater on a thermostat is the simpler and safer answer.
