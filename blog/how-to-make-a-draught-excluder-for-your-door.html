How to Make a Draught Excluder for Your Door: The 30-Minute Door Snake

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only — a simple home craft project; keep fabric fillings dry and away from heaters and open flames.

Of all the winter crafts, learning how to make a draught excluder for your door is the one that pays you back the same evening. The gap under a door is a letterbox lying down — a permanent slot pouring cold air across the floor at ankle height — and a weighted fabric snake, sitting snugly against it, stops the pour better than any amount of turning the heating up. The bought versions come in standard lengths that fit almost no real door exactly. Yours, made to measure from things already in the house, will fit yours perfectly and cost nothing but half an hour. Here is the no-sew method, the sewn method, and an honest comparison of what to stuff it with.

What a door snake can and cannot do

First, the honest boundary: a draught excluder seals one edge of the door — the bottom. It does nothing for the gaps around the frame, the letterbox or the keyhole, and those leak too. Our guide to stopping a draught through the front door works through every edge in order, and finding the draughts first tells you which edges your house actually leaks through. A snake's other limits are practical: it sits loose on the floor, so it needs nudging back after the door opens, and tired, under-filled snakes go flat and useless within weeks. Fill it firmly, make it slightly too long rather than too short, and it will do its one job properly all winter.

Measure and gather: materials from around the house

Measure the doorway first: the full width of the door opening, plus about 5cm (2 inches) extra at each end so the snake tucks into the corners. Write it down. Then gather:

  • For the casing: a pair of thick tights or leggings (no-sew method), or a rectangle of sturdy scrap fabric — an old curtain, a worn bedsheet, a shirt beyond wearing (sewn method)
  • For the filling: fabric scraps, old towels or T-shirts, cushion stuffing — or rice, dried beans or sand for weight (compared below)
  • Tools: scissors, a tape measure, a funnel or a rolled sheet of card for filling, and for the sewn version a needle and thread or a sewing machine
  • Optional: a handful of dried lavender among the scraps, if you like your practicalities scented

How to make a draught excluder for your door without sewing: the tights method

  1. Cut one leg from the tights or leggings, as high up as you can, giving you the longest tube. Thick, opaque pairs work best.
  2. Tie a tight knot in the toe end — two knots if the fabric is stretchy — and turn the leg so the knot sits inside.
  3. Fill it. Push fabric scraps in with the end of a wooden spoon, packing firmly and evenly, or pour rice or beans through the funnel. Aim for a snake that feels like a full sausage, not a sock with ambitions — it will settle and soften a little in use.
  4. Stop at your measured length, twist the open end, and knot it tightly — twice. Trim any excess fabric beyond the knots.
  5. Test it against the door. It should sit touching along the whole width with the ends pressed into the corners. Too short? The second leg is right there — many people make one for the front door and one for the back from a single pair.

The sewn version: neater, and properly washable

If you sew at all, the sewn snake takes twenty minutes and looks like a bought one. Cut a rectangle of fabric about 25cm wide and your measured door length plus a few centimetres for seams. Fold it lengthwise, right sides together, and stitch along the long edge and one short end to make a tube. Turn it right side out. Fill it as above — fabric scraps make the lightest, most washable filling; rice gives the most satisfying weight and drape. Fold the raw edges of the open end inward and stitch it closed, by hand or machine. The advantages over the tights version are real: a flat seam sits closer to the door, the casing survives washing, and a deliberate fabric — offcut velvet, old tweed, a cheerful print — makes the thing a small piece of the room rather than an apology at its feet. Either way, it pairs well with the other low-cost moves in our set of draught fixes under $25.

Fillings compared, honestly

Fabric scraps / old towelsMedium — pack firmlyFree, washable, uses up the unwearableToo light if loosely packed; bulk it up
Cushion stuffingLight — best in a snug casingSoft, even, easy to fillCan feel insubstantial on very draughty doors
RiceHeavy — drapes into gaps beautifullyThe best seal of all; cheap in bulkMust stay dry; damp rice goes musty and can attract pests — replace if it ever gets wet
Dried beans or lentilsHeavy — similar to riceEasy to pour, easy to findSame damp warning as rice; slightly lumpier drape
SandHeaviest — for the worst gapsNothing seals like itA split casing is a real mess — sew the sand into an inner bag first

The pragmatic verdict: start with fabric scraps, because they are free and forgiving, and graduate to rice for the one door — usually the front door — where the draught is strongest and the extra weight earns its keep. Whichever you choose, this is a dry-goods project: a snake that lives against a rain-wet doorstep will get damp whatever it is filled with, so shake it out and air it on the radiator side of the room now and then.

Fitting, care and safety

Fit is a habit, not an installation: the snake works when it is touching the door along its whole length, ends in the corners, so give it a nudge with your foot each time you close the door for the evening — it becomes automatic within a week. Keep it away from heaters, radiators' hottest faces and any open flame; it is, after all, a tube of fabric. Check the casing every so often for thinning and small splits, especially with rice or sand inside, and catch a leak early over a bin rather than across the hall carpet. With children or pets in the house, prefer fabric fillings and sturdy casings, and treat any split as a same-day repair. And one room to skip: the door snake belongs inside, at floor level, on doors you step over carefully — not across a top-of-stairs doorway or anywhere a loose tube underfoot in the dark is a trip waiting to happen. Fitted, filled and looked after, it becomes part of the room — the same finishing layer as the lamplight and textiles in our guide to making a living room feel cosy on a budget.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best filling for a homemade draught excluder?

For most doors, fabric scraps, old towels or the stuffing from a retired cushion: they are free, washable, and heavy enough once packed firmly. Rice and dried beans give the best weight and drape for draughty older doors, but must be kept dry and replaced if they ever get damp. Sand is the heavyweight option — effective, but messy if the casing splits, so double-bag it inside the tube if you use it.

How long should a door draught excluder be?

Measure the door itself rather than guessing: you want the excluder to cover the full width of the doorway plus about 5cm (2 inches) extra at each end, so it tucks into the frame corners where the worst of the draught sneaks through. A standard interior door is often around 76–86cm wide, but older and exterior doors vary a lot — the tape measure takes ten seconds and the fit is the whole point.

Can I make a draught excluder without sewing?

Yes — the tights method in this guide needs no needle at all. Cut one leg from a pair of thick tights or leggings, fill it firmly with fabric scraps or rice, and knot both ends tightly. It takes ten minutes, looks surprisingly tidy, and lasts a season or more. The sewn version is neater and properly washable, but the draught cannot tell the difference.

Will a draught excluder work on a big gap under the door?

A door snake handles the typical gap — a few millimetres up to roughly a centimetre — very well, because its weight settles it into the space. For a bigger gap, or a door you open and close constantly, a fixed brush strip or door sweep does the job better because it travels with the door. The two also combine happily: sweep on the busy door, snake on the quiet ones.

How do I wash a homemade draught excluder?

It depends on the filling, which is why washable fillings win. A fabric-scrap excluder with a sewn casing can be unpicked at one end, emptied, washed as a tube and refilled. A rice- or bean-filled one cannot be washed — spot-clean the casing, keep it dry, and treat the filling as seasonal: if it ever gets damp or musty, empty it out, wash the casing and refill fresh.
