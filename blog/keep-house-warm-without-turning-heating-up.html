How to Keep a House Warm Without Turning the Heating Up

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — what feels warm varies by home, health and weather; not a substitute for professional heating or health advice.

Learning how to keep a house warm without turning the heating up starts with a liberating thought: you do not have to make more heat to feel warmer. Most homes already produce enough warmth — they simply lose it too fast, spread it too thinly, or deliver it to empty rooms instead of the people in them. Fix those three leaks, in that order, and the same thermostat setting suddenly feels generous.

This guide works through the two halves of that idea. First, keep heat in: curtains, zoning and radiator habits. Second, warm the person: layers, targeted heat and small routines. Almost everything here is free; the few paid items are the cheap kind covered in our 10 draught fixes under $25.

Keep a house warm without turning the heating up: the mindset

Think of warmth as water in a bucket. Turning the heating up is pouring faster; the habits below are mending the holes and using a smaller bucket. A room also feels warmer or colder for reasons beyond the thermometer: cold air moving past skin, a chilly floor underfoot, and a cold window radiating coolness all drag comfort down at the same measured temperature. Stop the moving air, cover the cold surfaces, and a setting that felt mean starts to feel comfortable — which is exactly how the savings happen.

Use the sun by day, close the curtains at dusk

Sunlight through glass is free heat, so on bright winter days open curtains on sun-facing windows wide. The moment light fades, reverse it: close every curtain at dusk. Glass is one of the coldest surfaces in a room, and a closed curtain puts a layer of fabric — and a pocket of trapped, still air — between you and it.

Thermal or thick, lined curtains do this job best, especially if they hang close to the window, cover the frame, and reach the sill or the floor. A few habits sharpen the effect: tuck curtains behind radiators rather than over them where you can (more on that below), close curtains in unused rooms too, and hang a heavy curtain across a draughty front door. None of this adds heat — it stops tonight's heat escaping.

Zone the house: heat the rooms you are actually in

A whole-house temperature for a household sitting in one room is an expensive habit. Zoning is the free alternative: decide which rooms are in use this evening, and favour those. Close the doors to spare bedrooms, studies and hallways. Turn radiators down in unused rooms with their thermostatic valves (TRVs) if fitted, rather than heating empty air like the room with the sofa.

Two cautions keep zoning safe and sensible. First, turn unused radiators down, not fully off, during very cold spells: rooms left stone-cold risk condensation and damp on their walls, and in severe weather, pipes in unheated spaces can freeze. Second, a draught excluder or door snake at the base of the living-room door is what makes zoning real — without it, your warm room shares its heat with the hallway all evening. This is also the logic behind our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night: the smaller the space you keep warm, the less warmth it needs.

Rugs, floors and the cold under your feet

Bare boards, laminate and tile feel cold because they conduct heat away from feet quickly, and that chill colours how warm the whole room feels. A rug over the main sitting area changes the feel of a room out of all proportion to its cost — a second-hand rug does the job for very little. Smaller touches help too: a mat inside the front door, slippers kept by the sofa, and filler strips or a runner along gappy floorboards in older homes, where cold air rises between the boards.

Radiator habits that cost nothing

  • Do not block them. A sofa pushed against a radiator, or a long curtain draped over one, absorbs and traps the heat you have paid for. Leave a clear gap so warm air can circulate into the room.
  • Bleed them if they are patchy. A radiator that is hot at the bottom and cold at the top usually has trapped air inside. With the heating off and radiator cool, a radiator key lets the air hiss out until water appears — a five-minute job; check your heating manual if unsure.
  • Add foil behind radiators on outside walls. Reflector foil bounces heat back into the room instead of into the brickwork, for a few dollars in materials.
  • Keep the setting steady. Short, fierce blasts followed by cold spells usually feel worse — and rarely save anything — versus a steady, modest setting.

Warm the person, not just the house

The cheapest degree of warmth is the one worn. Add a layer before you touch the thermostat: a jumper over a T-shirt, warm socks or slippers, and a blanket or throw over legs on the sofa. Hot drinks warm hands at once, and moving — tidying, stretching, a short walk around the house — raises your own temperature as sitting still never does. None of this means enduring a cold home; a dressed, settled person simply needs the room a degree or two cooler than someone in thin clothes straight in from a chilly hallway.

Targeted warmth where you sit and sleep

When one spot needs to be properly warm — the sofa in the evening, the bed at night — heat that spot instead of the whole house. A heated throw over your legs, or an electric blanket pre-warming the bed, delivers comfort for a small fraction of the energy of whole-room heating. Our side-by-side guide to a heated throw vs an electric blanket explains which suits sofas and which suits beds, and our electric blanket vs space heater cost comparison shows the maths in full. Use them as the top layer of your strategy: house sealed and zoned first, targeted warmth second, thermostat last.

Small extras: cooking heat and ceiling fans

Ordinary cooking leaves residual warmth in a kitchen — a casserole cooling on the hob warms the room while you eat nearby, a pleasant bonus. Be clear about the line, though: never run an oven or cooker in order to heat your home, never leave cooking appliances on unattended for warmth, and never bring outdoor heaters, barbecues or charcoal indoors — they are a serious fire and carbon monoxide danger in enclosed spaces. If your home has a ceiling fan with a reverse (winter) setting, running it slowly in reverse can gently push warm air pooled at the ceiling back down into the room; if it creates a noticeable breeze, it is turning the wrong way or too fast.

The safety floor: warm enough is a real thing

Feeling warmer without more heating has a hard limit, and it matters. Never block boiler or furnace vents, air bricks or flues in the name of keeping heat in — fuel-burning appliances need that air, and blocking it risks carbon monoxide. Do not let any home stay dangerously cold for long periods: very cold rooms invite condensation, damp and mould, and in freezing weather, unprotected pipes can burst. Anyone who is elderly, very young, unwell or vulnerable may need a warmer home than the general guidance suggests. If staying warm is becoming a struggle, local councils, energy charities and health services can often help — using that support is part of keeping a house warm, too.

A simple evening routine

  1. As dusk falls, close all curtains, starting with the rooms in use.
  2. Close doors to unused rooms; turn their radiators down, not off.
  3. Put the door snake down, the rug-side slippers on, and a layer on.
  4. Settle with targeted warmth — throw on the sofa, blanket warming the bed.
  5. Check radiators are unblocked and the room feels still, not breezy.

Frequently asked questions

How can I make my house feel warmer without more heating?

Keep the heat you already have and warm yourself directly: close curtains at dusk, shut doors to unused rooms, put a rug on hard floors, and add a layer of clothing, warm socks and a hot drink. Then add targeted warmth where you sit or sleep, such as a heated throw, instead of raising the temperature of the whole house.

Do thermal curtains really help?

Yes, modestly. A thick or thermal-lined curtain adds an insulating layer and traps a pocket of still air between the fabric and a cold window, so the glass feels less chilling and less heat is lost that way. They work best closed at dusk, hanging close to the wall or window and long enough to cover the frame, and they are not a substitute for fixing large gaps.

Does closing doors keep a house warmer?

Yes. A closed door slows warm air moving into hallways, spare rooms and other spaces you are not using, so the room you are in holds its temperature better and feels warmer sooner. It works even better with a draught excluder at the base of the door. Keep a little ventilation in occupied rooms and never close off safety vents.

Is it cheaper to heat one room or the whole house?

Generally, heating the rooms you are actually using costs less than heating every room to the same temperature, because you are warming less air and fewer walls. Turn radiators down — not fully off in very cold weather — in unused rooms, close their doors, and concentrate warmth where people sit and sleep. Protect pipes and avoid letting any room become damp and very cold.

What temperature should a house be in winter?

As general guidance only, many households aim for around 18–21°C (64–70°F) in living rooms and a little cooler in bedrooms, but the right figure depends on health, age, the building and personal comfort. Vulnerable people may need warmer rooms. Do not let a home stay dangerously cold, and seek advice from a health professional or local guidance if you are unsure.
