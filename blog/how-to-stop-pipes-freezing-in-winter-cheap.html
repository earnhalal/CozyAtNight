How to Stop Pipes Freezing in Winter: Cheap Prevention That Actually Works

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only — commonly cited prevention guidance for US and Canadian homes; for anything structural or urgent, call a licensed plumber.

Learning how to stop pipes freezing in winter cheap is one of the best trades in home ownership: an afternoon of small, inexpensive jobs against one of the most expensive accidents a house can have. A burst pipe does not politely leak — it can empty the water system into your walls and ceilings until somebody turns it off, and the repair bill is the small part next to the drying, the plaster and the ruined floors. This checklist is written for US and Canadian homes, where winter does its worst, and every measure on it is cheap, reversible and doable by a homeowner with no special tools.

Why pipes freeze — and why they burst

Water is unusual stuff: when it freezes, it expands. In a pipe, that expansion has nowhere to go, and the ice that forms acts like a plug. The pressure builds in the water trapped between the ice plug and the closed tap or valve — and the pipe splits under that pressure, often not at the visible ice but somewhere along the trapped stretch. The flood then arrives at thaw, when the ice melts and the split opens. Two lessons hide in that mechanism, and the whole checklist follows from them: keep water in vulnerable pipes either moving or warm enough not to freeze, and know exactly where your main shutoff is before you ever need it in a hurry.

The how to stop pipes freezing in winter cheap checklist

  1. Find your main shutoff today. In the US this is often where the water line enters the house or at the meter; in Canada, commonly in the basement near the front wall. Turn it once to be sure it moves. Label it. Tell the household where it is. This step costs nothing and is worth more than everything else on the list combined.
  2. Lag every exposed pipe you can reach in unheated spaces — basement, crawl space, garage, attic — with foam pipe sleeves. They slit open, push on, and come pre-sized for common pipe diameters. Focus first on pipes near outside walls and vents.
  3. Seal the draughts where pipes live. Gaps around pipes where they pass through walls, and cracks near vents and foundations, pour cold air directly onto pipework. Seal them with foam or caulk as part of the same afternoon — the approach mirrors our guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up.
  4. Disconnect and drain outdoor hoses, and shut off and drain any outdoor taps that have an indoor isolation valve. A hose left attached traps water in the tap and the pipe behind it, splitting both in the first hard frost.
  5. On the coldest nights, drip the vulnerable taps. A slow, steady stream about the width of a pencil lead, from the cold tap furthest along the run where you can. Moving water resists freezing, and an open tap gives expanding ice somewhere to push.
  6. Open cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls at night, so warm room air reaches the pipes hiding behind them. In many kitchens and bathrooms, that closed cupboard is the coldest spot in the house — the same cold-trap problem our guide to the cheapest way to heat a bathroom works around.
  7. Hold the thermostat floor — never let the house fall below about 55°F (13°C) in cold weather, including when you travel. The next section explains why this figure is the one everyone cites.
  8. Insulate the water heater's pipes for the first stretch out of the tank, and check the tank's corner of the house is not the one cold spot you never visit.

The 55°F rule, explained

The figure you will see quoted by water utilities, insurers and engineers across the US and Canada is a minimum indoor temperature of about 55°F — roughly 13°C — below which the house should not be allowed to fall in cold weather. It is not a comfort recommendation; nobody is suggesting you live at 55°F. It is a safety floor with margin built in. Thermostats read the temperature in the hall, but pipes do not live in the hall — they live in cupboards, wall cavities, basements and the spaces behind baths, all of which run colder than the room the thermostat sits in. Holding the house at 55°F at its coldest, emptiest, nobody-home worst keeps even those hidden runs above freezing in most homes. If your house has known cold spots, bays over unheated garages, or a history of trouble, treat 55°F as the floor rather than the target and set the heating a little above it.

Pipes and places that freeze first

Not all pipework is equal, and a walk around your own house with this list will find your weak points in ten minutes. Pipes in unheated basements and crawl spaces are the classic casualty. Runs along outside walls, especially north-facing ones, come next, followed by pipes in garages, under kitchen and bathroom cabinets on external walls, and in the attic above insulation gaps. Homes that feel mysteriously cold in particular rooms — the diagnosis in our guide to why a house stays cold with the heating on — usually have cold pipework in the same places, because the same missing insulation is behind both problems. Lag and seal in this order of risk, and the checklist is mostly done in the places it matters.

Going away in winter: the extra steps

More bursts happen in empty houses than occupied ones, because nobody hears the drip and the heating is set low. Before a winter trip: set the thermostat at or above the 55°F floor — never off. Open the cabinet doors under every sink on an outside wall. Shut off the water at the main if you will be gone a long while and the house system allows it, and drain the taps after. Ask a neighbour or friend to look in every couple of days in deep cold, and make sure they know where the shutoff is too. The ten minutes this takes is the cheapest insurance policy in this entire article.

If a pipe freezes anyway — and if it bursts

Frozen but not burst: if a tap produces only a trickle in deep cold, suspect ice. Leave the tap open, keep the cabinet doors open, and warm the exposed pipe gently — a hairdryer on a low setting, warm towels, or a portable heater kept at a safe distance and watched. Never use a blowtorch or any open flame on a pipe; it is a fire risk and can boil the water in the pipe and burst it outright. Work from the tap back toward the blockage, slowly. If you cannot find the frozen stretch, or anything looks split or bulging, stop and call a plumber.

Burst: water off at the main shutoff, first, before towels, before photographs, before anything. Then electricity off to any area where water is near sockets or appliances, taps open to drain the system, belongings moved out of the water, and a plumber called. Photographs for insurance come after the water stops. The repair is a pipe; the disaster is the water, and the shutoff — which you found and labelled on a calm afternoon, per step one — is what decides which one you get.

Frequently asked questions

How cold does it have to be for pipes to freeze?

There is no single magic number, because it depends on where the pipe is and how long the cold lasts. The figure commonly cited by utilities and insurers is that unprotected pipes in unheated spaces become vulnerable when outdoor temperatures fall to around 20°F (about −6°C) and below, especially for hours at a time. A pipe inside a heated wall can survive colder weather than the same pipe in a draughty crawl space at milder temperatures — exposure and duration matter more than the headline number.

Should I really leave a tap dripping all night?

On the coldest nights, yes — for the taps served by pipes that run through unheated spaces or along outside walls. A slow, steady drip, about pencil-thin, keeps water moving so it has far less chance to freeze solid, and relieves pressure if ice does begin to form. It costs a little water for one night; a burst pipe costs a great deal more. Use the cold tap, and the one furthest from where water enters the house where you can.

What temperature should I leave the heating on when I go away in winter?

The commonly cited floor is about 55°F (roughly 13°C) — do not let the house fall below it while you are away in cold weather. That is warm enough to protect pipes in most homes, including the ones in cupboards and along outside walls, without heating an empty house to living temperature. If your home has known cold spots, set it a little higher and open the cabinet doors under sinks before you leave.

Does foam pipe lagging actually work?

Yes, within its limits. Foam sleeves slow down how fast a pipe loses heat, which buys time through a cold night and stops most freezes in spaces that only dip briefly below freezing. Lagging cannot save a pipe through days of deep cold in an unheated space by itself — that is what the thermostat floor, the dripping tap and sealing the draughts are for. Used together, the cheap measures cover almost every ordinary winter.

What do I do first if a pipe bursts?

Turn off the water at the main shutoff — the stopcock — before anything else. Then switch off electricity to any area where water is near sockets or appliances, open the taps to drain what is left, move what you can out of the water, and call a plumber. Photograph the damage for your insurer once the water is stopped. Speed with the shutoff is the whole game: minutes of flooding do far more damage than the pipe repair costs.
