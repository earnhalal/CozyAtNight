How Much Does a Halogen Heater Cost to Run Per Hour?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

If you are asking how much does a halogen heater cost to run per hour, the short answer for a typical UK model is about 9.6p on one bar, 19.2p on two bars and 28.8p with all three bars on, using the illustrative rate in this guide. That makes it one of the cheapest electric heaters to run hour by hour — but only for a very specific job: warming one person, in one spot, for a short time.

If you are weighing up the wider picture, start with our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night.

How much does a halogen heater cost to run per hour? The table

The formula never changes: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A halogen heater's power depends on how many bars are switched on, so the cost rises in clear steps rather than on a dial.

Worked example — one clearly illustrative UK rate. We will use 24p per kWh as an example UK price-cap-style rate. It is not a promise about your bill: tariffs change and your own deal may be higher or lower. Read the unit rate on your own bill and use the same formula for your real answer.

A brief US note. Halogen heaters are less common in the US, where the same maths applies at our illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh: about 6.4 cents per hour on one 400W bar, about 12.8 cents on two bars, and about 19.2 cents on three bars. Your state rate will differ, so substitute your own price per kWh in the formula.

How halogen bars work

Inside the heater, each glowing bar is a separate heating element with a set wattage. On many typical UK models each bar is around 400W, so a common 1,200W-class heater is simply three 400W bars in one case, and the switches let you run one, two or all three. Treat those figures as typical examples, not a standard: some models use different bar sizes, so check the wattage printed on your heater's label or box.

Behind the bars sits a reflective backing that pushes the heat forwards, and in front there should always be a guard. Many models also oscillate, swinging slowly from side to side to spread the beam across a slightly wider arc.

Radiant heat: warm in seconds, gone in seconds

A halogen heater does not mainly warm air. It produces radiant heat, which travels in a straight line and warms people and objects it lands on, much as sunlight warms your face on a cold day. Sit in front of it and you can feel the effect within moments, even if the room itself still feels chilly.

That line-of-sight rule is the key limit. Turn a chair away, move to the other side of the room, or let furniture block the beam, and the warmth largely disappears.

Where a halogen heater fits best

Used in the right niche, that beam is genuinely useful. A halogen heater suits one person sitting fairly still in a small space: reading in one chair, working at a desk, or taking the edge off a chilly corner for an hour or two while the main heating stays low.

It is a poor fit for heating a whole family room evenly, warming a bedroom before sleep, or holding a room's temperature through the night. The larger and draughtier the space, and the more people spread around it, the more the line-of-sight limit shows. Halogen vs oil-filled radiator vs fan heater: the honest comparison

An oil-filled radiator warms more slowly, because its element first heats oil inside the fins, but that stored heat keeps radiating after switch-off and spreads more evenly around a room. Our companion guide explains how much it costs to run an oil-filled radiator per hour with the same formula, so you can compare like with like. At a higher wattage setting an oil-filled radiator usually costs more per hour than one halogen bar, but it is doing the bigger job of warming the room itself.

A fan heater pushes warmed air around with a fan, so it raises a small room's air temperature faster than a halogen beam and reaches people who are not sitting directly in front of it. At exactly the same wattage, all three heaters cost exactly the same to run — electricity is billed by the kilowatt-hour. The real differences are where the heat goes, how long it lingers, how noisy it is, and how it needs to be used safely.

The drawbacks nobody mentions at the till

The first is the light itself. Glowing bars are bright, and many people find the orange glare tiring in a dim evening room or distracting while watching a screen.

The third is stability. Many halogen heaters are tall and relatively light for their height, so a knock, a trailing cable or an unsteady floor can tip one over. Good models have a tip-over switch that cuts power if they fall, but that is a back-up, not a reason to place one where it is likely to be knocked in the first place.

Safety: read this before you switch one on

Safety first. Keep a halogen heater well clear of furniture, curtains, bedding, clothing and anything that could fall onto it, and never drape anything over it. Keep the guard in place and never use a heater with a damaged guard, cable or plug.

Never leave a halogen heater unattended, and never use one while sleeping or in bed. The bars run extremely hot, and a sleeping household cannot notice a knock, a fallen cushion or a curtain drifting too close. Plug the heater directly into a wall socket rather than an extension lead, place it on a flat, stable surface away from walkways, and keep children and pets from touching the guard. Switch it off and let it cool before moving it or putting it away. If a heater cuts out, smells unusual or shows damage, stop using it and follow the manufacturer's guidance.

How to keep the cost down in practice

Start on one bar and add a second only if you still feel cold after a few minutes in the beam. Sit within the beam rather than heating an empty side of the room, close the door, and deal with obvious draughts so the warmth you feel is not fighting cold air at your ankles. Use the heater in bursts while you are awake and present, then switch it off when you leave or go to bed.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a halogen heater cost to run per hour on one bar?

On one bar, a typical halogen heater uses around 400W, or 0.4kW. At the illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh used in this guide, that is 0.4 × 24p, which is about 9.6p per hour. On two bars it is about 19.2p, and on three bars about 28.8p.

Is a halogen heater cheaper to run than a fan heater?

Per hour on its lowest setting, usually yes, because one halogen bar at around 400W uses less power than a typical fan heater on a higher setting. At the same wattage, though, the cost is the same: electricity is charged by the kilowatt-hour, whatever appliance uses it.

Does a halogen heater warm a whole room?

Not in the way a convector, fan heater or oil-filled radiator does. A halogen heater sends radiant heat in a beam, so people and objects directly in front of it feel warm quickly, while air and corners outside that beam stay much cooler, especially in a larger or draughty room.

Can you leave a halogen heater on overnight?

No. Do not leave a halogen heater unattended, and do not use one while sleeping. The bars run very hot, the heater can be knocked over, and bedding, curtains or furniture nearby create a fire risk. Switch it off before bed and use a safer overnight option instead.

Why does a halogen heater feel warm so quickly?

Because it uses radiant heat rather than waiting to warm all the air in a room first. The glowing bars send heat straight to whatever is in front of them, a bit like sunshine on a cold day. That is also why the warmth stops almost as soon as you switch it off.
