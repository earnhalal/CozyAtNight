Radiator Reflector Foil — Does It Work, or Is It a Waste of Money?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — not heating-engineering advice. Savings figures quoted anywhere for foil vary widely; this guide explains the effect rather than promising a number.

Search for radiator reflector foil does it work and you will find two confident camps: sellers promising dramatic savings, and sceptics calling it a gimmick. The honest answer sits between them. Reflector foil does something real — it reflects radiant heat that would otherwise soak into the wall behind a radiator — but the effect is small, and it only exists where the wall behind the radiator is an outside wall losing heat to the outdoors. Fitted there, neatly, it is a cheap job with a modest payoff. Fitted behind radiators on internal walls, it does next to nothing.

This guide explains the physics in plain terms, where foil earns its keep, how the savings claims should be read, and how to fit it so it actually stays reflective. It sits alongside the other radiator checks in our diagnosis of why a house stays cold even with the heating on.

How a radiator actually heats a room

Despite the name, most of a radiator's output is convection: it warms air, which rises, circulates and falls as it cools. But a meaningful share is radiant heat — infrared energy travelling in straight lines, including directly backwards into the wall the radiator hangs on. If that wall is an internal wall, the absorbed heat mostly stays in the house and eventually finds its way back. If it is an outside wall, especially an uninsulated solid wall, some of that heat conducts through and is lost outside. That backwards radiant share, on that specific wall, is the only heat reflector foil can rescue.

Radiator reflector foil does it work — where, and how much?

On an outside wall, a clean reflective surface behind the radiator bounces a portion of the radiant heat back into the room instead of letting the wall absorb it, and the wall itself stays slightly cooler, which slows conduction a little further. Energy charities and consumer guides that have looked at foil generally describe the effect in the same terms: real, but modest — a small saving on the heating bill rather than a transformation, and the largest gains in homes with solid, uninsulated walls. Where a home already has cavity-wall insulation, the wall was losing less to begin with, so there is less for the foil to save. Treat any precise percentage printed on packaging as marketing, not measurement: the true figure depends on your wall, your radiator temperatures and how much of the wall the foil actually covers.

On internal walls — between two heated rooms — there is no outdoor loss to prevent, and foil is essentially decoration. The honest rule: foil behind radiators on outside walls only, and expect a small win, not a miracle.

What to use, and what to skip

Purpose-made reflector panels and foil-faced bubble sheets are sold for this job; the material matters less than the surface — smooth, shiny and clean reflects, while dull or dusty surfaces reflect far less. Ordinary kitchen foil is sometimes suggested as a free alternative, but it tears, crinkles (which scatters rather than reflects) and dulls quickly; it is a poor substitute for a panel made to hang flat for years. Whatever you use, it must be non-flammable-rated for use behind a radiator and fitted according to its instructions — a radiator is a hot appliance, and anything behind it should be made for the location.

Fitting it so it works

The job is simple and usually needs no tools beyond scissors and a tape measure. With the radiator cold, measure the area between the brackets and cut the panel slightly smaller than the radiator so it stays hidden. Slide it down behind the radiator, shiny side facing into the room, keeping it flat and clear of the valves and pipework; many panels come with adhesive pads or magnetic strips, and gravity does most of the work on a well-cut piece. Cover as much of the wall area directly behind the radiator as you can — reflections only happen where the foil actually is. If the radiator would need to come off the wall to fit it, that is a plumber's job involving drained pipework; slide-in fitting exists precisely to avoid it.

While you are behind there, check the basics from our wider guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up: is the radiator bled, is furniture blocking it, are curtains trapping its heat against the glass? Foil is the garnish on those fundamentals, never a substitute for them.

The verdict

Worth it: radiators on uninsulated outside walls, in a home you will stay in, fitted neatly with proper panels — a low-cost job with a small, real return that repeats every winter. Not worth it: internal walls, insulated walls that lose little anyway, or as a substitute for draught-proofing, bleeding and balancing, which return far more. If a seller's savings claim is doing the persuading, walk away; the physics, not the packaging, is the reason to fit it.

Mistakes that quietly kill the effect

Three fitting mistakes undo the modest gain. The first is a dirty or dusty surface: foil reflects when it is clean and smooth, and a panel that has gathered a winter of dust behind the radiator reflects far less — wipe it when you bleed the radiators each autumn. The second is partial coverage, a narrow strip centred behind the radiator while heat radiates across the whole wall area behind it; cover the area the radiator covers. The third is fitting foil and then pushing the sofa back against the radiator, blocking the convection that does most of the heating in the first place.

It also helps to rank foil honestly among the other radiator wins. Bleeding a radiator that is cold at the top returns a whole panel of heat for ten minutes' work — our step-by-step for how to bleed a radiator cold at the top covers it. Balancing a system so every radiator gets its share changes whole rooms. Foil comes after those, as the small extra on outside walls. Done in that order, each job is worth its effort; done in reverse, foil gets blamed for not performing miracles it never promised in this guide, whatever the packaging said.

One more honest test if you want evidence from your own home: fit foil behind one outside-wall radiator, leave a similar one bare, and compare how the wall behind each feels after an evening of heating — the foil-protected wall should feel cooler to the touch, because less heat is soaking into it.

Frequently asked questions

Radiator reflector foil does it work at all?

Yes, within limits. A reflective panel behind a radiator reflects radiant heat that would otherwise be absorbed by the wall. The effect is real but modest, and it only matters on outside walls — ideally uninsulated ones — where absorbed heat is genuinely lost to the outdoors.

Does reflector foil work behind radiators on internal walls?

Not meaningfully. Heat absorbed by an internal wall stays inside the house and redistributes between rooms, so there is little loss for the foil to prevent. Save the material and effort for radiators mounted on outside walls.

How much money does radiator foil save?

No single figure is honest for every home: it depends on the wall construction, insulation, radiator temperature and coverage. Energy charities and consumer guides generally describe savings as small but real on uninsulated outside walls. Treat precise percentages on packaging as marketing rather than measurement.

Can I use ordinary kitchen foil behind a radiator?

It is a poor substitute. Kitchen foil tears, crinkles — which scatters heat instead of reflecting it cleanly — and dulls with dust and time. Purpose-made reflector panels are made to hang flat, stay reflective and be rated for use behind a hot radiator.

Do I need to remove the radiator to fit reflector foil?

Usually not. Most panels are cut slightly smaller than the radiator and slid down behind it, shiny side facing the room, held by adhesive pads or magnetic strips. If fitting would require removing the radiator from the wall, that involves pipework and is a job for a plumber.
