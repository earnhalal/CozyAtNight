Heated Throw vs Electric Blanket: Which One Do You Actually Need?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Wattage ranges below are typical examples, not promises about a specific product — always check the label and instructions for the one you own or are considering.

The heated throw vs electric blanket question sounds like a shopping detail, but it is really a question about where you get cold. If your coldest moment is an evening on the sofa, the answer is different from a bed that feels like a fridge at midnight. Both use far less electricity than heating a room, as our comparison of an electric blanket vs a space heater and its running costs shows — but they are built for different places, different hours and different habits.

Below we define each one, compare them head to head, work out running costs with the same honest formula we use across this site, and finish with a simple decision guide.

First, the definitions: what each one actually is

What is a heated throw?

A heated throw is an overblanket: a soft, heated layer designed to be draped over you, or wrapped around you, while you sit or recline. Think sofa, armchair, reading corner or a desk chair on a working-from-home day. It is deliberately portable — you plug it in where you are and take it with you when you move. It is sized for a person, not for a piece of furniture, and it is normally used while you are awake.

What is an electric blanket?

An electric blanket is made for a bed. Most commonly it is an underblanket: a fitted, bed-sized layer that goes on the mattress, under your bottom sheet, and warms the bed itself. Some are overblankets instead, sized like a duvet layer to lie over you in bed. Electric blankets come in single, double and king sizes, and double sizes may offer dual controls, so each side of the bed can run at a different warmth — or one side can be off entirely.

Read the wattage row carefully, because it surprises people: the two are in the same low range. These are typical ranges only — check the label, because sizes and settings vary. Neither is in the league of a space heater, which is why both can warm a person rather than a room, a principle we return to in our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night.

What does each one cost to run?

The formula is the same one we use everywhere on CozyAtNight: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A 120W heated throw is 0.12 kilowatts. A 100W electric blanket is 0.1 kilowatts. To turn that into money, we use two clearly illustrative example rates — 16 US cents per kWh and 24p per kWh in the UK — exactly as in our blanket-versus-heater guide. Neither rate is a promise about your bill; use the rate on your own bill for your real figure.

At those examples, the 120W throw costs about 1.9 cents, or about 2.9p, per hour at full power. The 100W blanket costs about 1.6 cents, or about 2.4p, per hour. In other words, both are pennies per hour. A blanket run on a low setting overnight will usually cost less per hour than its maximum figure, while a throw used for a short evening burst may run for less time overall.

The honest conclusion is that running cost rarely decides this choice. The gap between them is tiny compared with the gap between either and a heater — for that comparison in full, read how an electric blanket compares with a space heater on cost and safety. What decides it is the job: where you sit, where you sleep, and whether anyone shares the warmth.

Washing and care: what to expect from both

Many throws and blankets are washable, but only in the way their own label describes. The general pattern is to detach the controller and cable first, follow the stated temperature and cycle exactly, and let the item dry completely before reconnecting it or switching it on. If a label says spot-clean only, that instruction wins over any general advice, including this paragraph.

Storage matters too. Store heated bedding flat, or rolled or folded loosely, rather than crushed under heavy items, which can damage internal wiring. Keep it dry, and give the cable, plug and fabric a slow look-over at the start of each season. Scorch marks, fraying, exposed wiring or a damaged controller mean the item should be replaced, not repaired at home.

The decision guide: which one do you actually need?

Choose a heated throw if…

Your cold hours happen while you are awake. You spend evenings on the sofa or in one chair, you work at a desk and feel the chill in your lap and shoulders, or you want warmth you can carry between rooms. A throw is also the simpler choice if your bed is already comfortable and the problem is purely the living room in the evening.

Choose an electric blanket if…

The cold bed itself is the problem. If sheets feel icy when you get in, if you wake cold at 3 a.m., or if you want to pre-warm the bed and then sleep on a low setting, that is blanket territory. Choose one, too, if you share a bed with a partner who likes different warmth: dual controls let each side be set separately, which no throw can do. If that sounds like you, our guide on how to choose an electric blanket, including sizes, controls and safety features walks through the details.

Could you need both — or neither?

Some households genuinely use both: a throw for the evening, a blanket for the night, and the main heating turned down for both. Others need neither — a warmer duvet, an extra ordinary blanket and a hot water bottle solve plenty of cold-bed problems without a plug. The test is always the same: will this let you turn something bigger down? If yes, it earns its keep. If it runs on top of a fully heated home, it is an extra cost, however small.

Safety rules that apply to both

Look for automatic shut-off and overheat protection, and treat them as essentials rather than extras. Never fold, bunch or trap a throw or blanket while it is switched on, because concentrated heat can damage the elements inside. Never use a heated item in a way its instructions forbid, keep both types dry, and plug them in as the manufacturer directs. Inspect fabric, cables and controllers each season, and stop using anything damaged.

Finally, some people should not use heated bedding at all, or should only use it as the manufacturer directs — instructions commonly include guidance for babies and young children, people who are insensitive to heat, and certain medical situations. The wording that matters is the wording supplied with your product. Read it, and follow it over anything online, including this guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a heated throw and an electric blanket?

A heated throw is an overblanket made to be worn or draped over you on a sofa, chair or at a desk, and it is portable between rooms. An electric blanket is made for a bed: most often an underblanket fitted to the mattress, or sometimes a bed-sized overblanket, sized for a single, double or king bed. The throw follows you around the home; the blanket stays with the bed.

Which is cheaper to run, a heated throw or an electric blanket?

They are close, because both use low wattage compared with a space heater. A heated throw typically uses about 100–160W and an electric blanket about 50–150W, so a blanket on a low overnight setting is often slightly cheaper per hour, while a throw used for an hour or two on the sofa costs pennies as well. At the illustrative rates in this guide, both cost roughly 2–4 cents or pence per hour at full power.

Can I use a heated throw in bed?

Only if the manufacturer says so. Most heated throws are designed to be draped over you while you are awake, not to be slept under or lain on all night, unless the instructions explicitly state that bed use is allowed. If you want warmth in bed, an electric blanket made for that purpose — especially one designed to pre-warm the bed or run on low overnight — is the appropriate choice.

Can you wash a heated throw?

Many heated throws are washable, but only in the way their label describes. In general you detach the controller and cable first, follow the temperature and cycle instructions on the care label exactly, and let the throw dry completely before reconnecting or using it. If the label says not to wash, or to spot-clean only, follow that instead. The same detach-and-follow-the-label rule applies to electric blankets.

Is a heated throw worth it?

For many people, yes — if your coldest hours are evenings on the sofa, reading, watching television or working at a desk, a heated throw warms you directly for pennies per hour and can let you turn the room heating down. It is less worthwhile if your main problem is a cold bed at night, because that is the job an electric blanket is designed for. The worth comes from using it instead of heating a whole room, not on top of it.
