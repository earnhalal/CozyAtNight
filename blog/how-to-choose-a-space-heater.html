How to Choose a Space Heater: Wattage, Safety Features and Running Costs

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · This is a buying guide — no product is recommended or sponsored. General guidance only; always follow the manufacturer instructions and local fire-safety guidance.

Learning how to choose a space heater starts with two numbers and one rule. The numbers are your room size and the heater's wattage. The rule is safety: features and placement matter more than any extra setting, because a heater needs clear space and a proper socket.

This guide works through wattage first, then heater types in general terms, the safety features and placement rules that are not optional, and finally the simple maths that tells you what any heater will cost to run.

How to choose a space heater by wattage: match the heater to the room

An undersized heater runs at full power and still leaves a room cold. An oversized one cycles on and off uncomfortably. The widely used rule of thumb is about 10 watts per square foot — roughly 100 watts per square metre — for a room heated by electricity alone, with standard ceiling heights and ordinary insulation.

So a small bedroom of about 100 square feet (around 9–10 square metres) points to roughly 1,000W, while a living room of about 150 square feet (around 14 square metres) points to roughly 1,500W. If the heater is only topping up existing heating, allow less than the full rule of thumb. High ceilings, large windows and poor insulation push the need up; a small, well-sealed room pushes it down.

There is also a practical ceiling. Most plug-in models in North America top out at about 1,500W, while models sold for UK sockets are commonly rated up to about 2,000–2,500W. If your room needs more than one heater can give, that usually means its draughts need fixing or its main heating should be used instead — see the circuit rules below before considering a second heater.

Heater types, in general terms: how each one feels

Fan and ceramic heaters: fast warmth, some noise

These blow air over a heated element, so warmth arrives quickly and spreads around a room. They suit short bursts — a desk area while you work, or warming a room before a routine. The trade-off is the fan: you will hear it, and moving air can feel draughty once the heat cuts out. Ceramic elements moderate their own temperature to a degree, but that does not replace the safety features below.

Oil-filled radiators: slow, silent, and warm after switch-off

An oil-filled radiator heats sealed oil, which heats the body, which warms the room gently. It is slow to get going, but near silent, has no fan to stir dust, and keeps giving off heat after it switches off. That steady character suits longer use while people are awake — though it does not make any heater safe to leave running overnight.

Radiant and infrared heaters: warmth in line of sight

Radiant heaters warm objects and people directly in front of them, rather than heating all the air first — closer to sunshine on a cold day. They feel instant in the beam and do little for a room's far corners. Their exposed glowing elements make clear placement especially important.

Panel and micathermic heaters: slim and gentle

Panel-style heaters are thin, often wall-mountable, and give gentle background heat without a fan. Micathermic models combine radiant and convection heat in a slim body and warm up faster than oil-filled designs. Both suit steady, quiet topping-up rather than a fast blast of heat.

Safety features: the non-negotiables

Whatever type you choose, treat the following as requirements, not upgrades.

Tip-over switch. This cuts power if the heater is knocked over — by a pet, a child, or a trailing cable. Overheat protection cuts power if the heater gets too hot, for example because its airflow is blocked. A recognised safety certification or listing for your region shows the model was assessed against local safety standards; buy from a reputable seller.

Cool-touch housing matters where children or pets can brush against the heater, because some bodies get hot enough to burn on contact. A thermostat holds a set temperature instead of running flat out, and a timer lets you schedule a warm-up and automatic switch-off — both keep running time, and therefore cost, under control.

Placement rules: where the heater sits matters as much as what it is

Place a space heater on a flat, hard floor — never on a bed, sofa, deep carpet or wobbly table. Keep at least about 3 feet, roughly 1 metre, of clear space between the heater and bedding, curtains, furniture, paper, clothing and anything else that can burn. That distance is widely cited in fire-service guidance, and it applies on every side.

Plug the heater directly into a wall socket — never into an extension lead, power strip or adaptor, which may not be rated for its sustained load. Never run two high-wattage heaters on one circuit, and unplug it when not in use. Keep heaters away from water, and only use one near a bathroom if the model is explicitly rated for bathroom use.

Finally, never leave a space heater running unattended, and do not run one overnight. That verdict is central to our electric blanket versus space heater comparison: heating the bed itself is cheaper and safer overnight for most people, while a heater does its work in the evening, with someone awake.

Running costs: a watt is a watt

The formula never changes: cost per hour = kilowatts × your price per kWh. A 1,500W heater is 1.5kW, so at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh it costs about 24 cents per hour at full power; at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, about 36p per hour. Those rates are clearly labelled examples — check your own bill for your real price.

Here is the honest point the marketing tends to blur: all plug-in resistive heaters — fan, ceramic, oil-filled, radiant and panel alike — use their rated kilowatts at full power. A watt of electricity becomes the same amount of heat whatever the heater cost to buy, so no model is magically more efficient at full power. Real cost differences come from the thermostat switching it off once the room is warm, a timer stopping it running too long, and heating a smaller, better-sealed space. Draughts waste more heat than any heater choice can save, which is why our list of simple draught fixes for doors and windows pairs well with any heater, and why our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night starts with sealing the room.

Noise: the feature you only notice at night

If the heater will run in a bedroom or beside a desk, think about sound. Fan models hum and click as they cycle; radiant models can tick as they expand and contract; oil-filled radiators are usually the quietest. There is no standard noise test across brands, so treat any stated figure cautiously, and remember a correctly sized heater cycles less frantically than an undersized one straining at full power.

Frequently asked questions

What size space heater do I need for a room?

As a rule of thumb, allow about 10 watts per square foot, or roughly 100 watts per square metre, for a room heated by electricity alone with standard ceiling heights — less if the heater is only topping up existing heating. A small bedroom might need about 1,000W, while a larger living room can reach the typical plug-in maximum of about 1,500W in North America or up to about 2,000–2,500W on UK sockets.

What safety features should a space heater have?

Treat these as non-negotiable: a tip-over switch that cuts power if the heater falls, overheat protection, a recognised safety certification or listing for your region, a thermostat and a timer. Where children or pets are around, add a cool-touch housing. Then follow the placement rules: flat hard floor, clear space around it, and plugged directly into a wall socket.

How much does it cost to run a space heater?

Use this formula: kilowatts multiplied by your price per kilowatt-hour, multiplied by the hours it runs. A 1,500W heater is 1.5kW, so at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh it costs about 24 cents per hour at full power; at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh it is about 36p per hour. A thermostat cycling on and off usually brings the real average down.

Can you leave a space heater on overnight?

Generally, no — leaving a space heater running unsupervised overnight is not recommended. Sleeping counts as unsupervised, and a household cannot guarantee the clear space around the heater stays clear all night. Warm the room before bed, switch the heater off, and use a safer overnight option for the bed itself if you still feel cold.

Are expensive space heaters more efficient?

At full power, a watt is a watt: all plug-in resistive heaters turn their rated electricity into heat at the same rate, whatever the marketing claims. Differences between models are in heat delivery, thermostat and timer controls, noise and build quality — and those controls, plus how long you run the heater, are what actually change your bill.
