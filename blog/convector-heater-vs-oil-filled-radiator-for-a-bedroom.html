Convector Heater vs Oil Filled Radiator for a Bedroom: Which Belongs in Yours?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Electricity prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

Choosing between a convector heater vs oil filled radiator for a bedroom is not really about which heater is stronger. Both can warm a small room. The real difference is timing: a convector heats the air directly and you feel it sooner, while an oil filled radiator starts slowly, then holds a steadier warmth and keeps radiating after switch-off. For sleep, that difference — plus noise, moving air and safety around bedding — matters more than any headline wattage.

Convector heater vs oil filled radiator for a bedroom: the short verdict

Pick a convector if your routine is short: take the chill off while you change or read, then switch off. Pick an oil filled radiator if your routine is long: an evening in the room, a slow pre-warm, and warmth that fades gradually rather than stopping at the switch.

How each one actually heats the room

A convector draws cool air in at the bottom, passes it over a hot element, and lets the warmed air rise out of the top. That air circulates, so warmth spreads — but air cools quickly. Open a door, or switch off, and the benefit leaves with it.

An oil filled radiator heats sealed oil inside its fins. The oil warms the metal body, which radiates heat gently into the room. Nothing is blown anywhere. It is slower to begin, because the oil and metal heat up first, but that stored heat is why it keeps giving off warmth after switch-off. Our guide to how long an oil filled radiator takes to heat a room covers that delay.

Heat-up speed, and what happens when you switch off

For a quick pre-warm, the convector wins clearly. You feel moving warm air within minutes, and a small bedroom can lose its chill well before an oil filled radiator has got going. If you come home late, that speed is a genuine advantage.

For staying warm, the picture reverses. A convector's warmth is only there while it runs; switch off and the room cools quickly, especially if it is draughty. An oil filled radiator fades gradually, which suits a room you heat before bed and then let coast.

Noise, air movement, dust and the dry-air feel

A plain convector with no fan is silent in itself: there is no motor to hum. An oil filled radiator is also near-silent, apart from occasional soft clicks as the metal expands and contracts. If quiet is your priority, avoid assuming either type is noisy — but check carefully whether a convector includes a fan booster, because that version will be audible.

Air movement is where they part company. A convector works by moving air, so it stirs the room continuously. That can lift and circulate dust, which some sleepers notice, and moving warm air can feel drying over a long evening. An oil filled radiator moves far less air, so the room often feels calmer and less dry — although any heater can make a room feel drier, and neither type adds moisture.

Surface temperature, children and pets

Both types get hot to the touch in use, just in different patterns. A convector's casing and grille can become hot, and the air leaving it is hot. An oil filled radiator's fins become warm to hot across a broad surface that a child or pet can lean against. Treat both with the same general caution: place them where small hands and animals cannot easily reach or knock them, supervise in rooms used by children, and never assume a surface is safe to touch because the heater looks gentle. Follow the manufacturer's guidance for your specific model.

Space and footprint in a small bedroom

Convectors are usually slim. Many are light, hang on a wall, or stand close against it, so they suit a small bedroom where floor space is tight — provided the required clearances are still kept. Oil filled radiators are bulkier and heavier, with fins that need air around them, and they take a noticeable patch of floor. They can be wheeled away in the morning, but in a very small room that patch may be exactly where you need to walk. Measure the space, including clearance from the bed, before you decide.

Running costs: same wattage means the same full-power cost

Here is the honest part many comparisons avoid. At the same wattage, a convector and an oil filled radiator cost exactly the same per hour at full power, because electricity is billed by the kilowatt-hour and both turn it into heat at effectively the same rate. The formula never changes: cost = kW × rate. A 2kW heater at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh costs 2 × 24p = 48p per hour; at an illustrative US rate of 16¢ per kWh it costs 2 × 16¢ = 32¢ per hour. A 1.5kW model costs 36p or 24¢ on the same illustrative rates.

Illustrative rates, clearly labelled. The UK 24p per kWh and US 16¢ per kWh figures above are examples only, not your tariff. Find the per-kWh price on your own bill and use the same formula. Our breakdown of the oil filled radiator vs fan heater running costs applies the same honesty to another common pairing.

Real totals can still differ between the two, just not because of the hourly ceiling. They differ because of the thermostat duty cycle: how much of each hour the heater actually spends drawing full power once the room is warm. A radiator that coasts after switch-off may cycle less in a well-sealed room used all evening. A convector in a draughty room may run almost constantly. Room heat loss — draughts, thin walls, cold windows — decides more of your bill than heater style does, which is why our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night starts with the room, not the appliance.

Which suits which sleeper and which room?

The quick pre-warmer. You only heat the bedroom before bed, then sleep without it. A convector's fast warm air fits that short job best.

The all-evening room user. You read, work or watch in the bedroom for hours. An oil filled radiator's steadier, stiller heat is usually the more comfortable match.

The light or dust-sensitive sleeper. Choose still air: an oil filled radiator, or a fan-free convector used only before bed. Avoid fan-assisted models where you sleep.

The small or draughty bedroom. In a small, well-sealed room, either works — pick on footprint. In a draughty room, fix the draughts first; otherwise both run longer, and the convector's warm air escapes fastest. Our guide on how to choose a space heater covers sizing and safety features.

Safety rules for either heater in a bedroom

Bedrooms ask for extra care because the heater shares space with soft, flammable things. Keep clear space on all sides, well away from bedding, curtains, clothes and anything that could fall onto it. Plug the heater directly into a wall socket — never an extension lead or multi-way adaptor. Never cover a heater, dry anything on it, or use it with a damaged cable. Use it overnight only as the maker allows: if the manufacturer's instructions do not explicitly permit the overnight use you have in mind, warm the room before bed and switch it off. Choose tip-over and overheat protection, and keep the heater where children and pets cannot knock it over.

Frequently asked questions

Is a convector heater or an oil filled radiator better for a bedroom?

Neither suits every bedroom. A convector warms the air faster, which helps for a quick pre-warm. An oil filled radiator is slower but steadier and keeps radiating after switch-off, which suits a longer evening or night routine. Choose by timing, noise sensitivity and how quickly your room loses heat.

Which heats a bedroom faster, a convector or an oil filled radiator?

A convector usually feels faster because it heats the air directly and that warm air circulates straight away. An oil filled radiator must first heat the oil inside it, so the start is slower, although it then gives a steadier warmth and continues radiating for a while after it is switched off.

Which is cheaper to run in a bedroom, a convector or an oil filled radiator?

At the same wattage, neither is cheaper per hour at full power. Cost equals kilowatts multiplied by your price per kWh, so a 2kW model costs 48p an hour at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh, or 32 cents at an illustrative US rate of 16 cents. Real totals depend on thermostat cycling and room heat loss.

Is an oil filled radiator quieter than a convector heater for sleeping?

Both can be near-silent because a plain convector and an oil filled radiator have no fan. An oil filled radiator may make occasional soft clicks as metal expands and contracts. A convector with a fan is a different case and will be noisier. Check the exact type before assuming any heater is silent.

Can you leave a convector heater or oil filled radiator on overnight in a bedroom?

Only use either heater overnight if the manufacturer's instructions explicitly allow that use, and only with clear space around it, a direct wall socket and nothing covering it. If the instructions do not allow overnight use, warm the room before bed and switch it off. Never cover a heater or leave bedding or curtains against it.
