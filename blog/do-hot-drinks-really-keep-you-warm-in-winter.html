Do Hot Drinks Really Keep You Warm in Winter? The Honest Answer

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only — not medical advice. Feeling warm and being warm are different things, and this guide keeps them separate.

Do hot drinks really keep you warm in winter? The honest answer is: a little, briefly, and mostly in the places that notice it most — your mouth, your hands and your mood. A mug of tea is not going to raise your core temperature in any way that matters, and anyone who tells you a hot toddy will keep you warm on a freezing night has it backwards. But the feeling a hot drink gives you is not a trick or a placebo to be dismissed. It has real mechanisms behind it, and on a cold evening those mechanisms are worth understanding — and enjoying with your eyes open.

What a mug of heat actually adds up to

Start with the arithmetic, because it settles the scale of the claim. Your body is not a cold object waiting to be heated; it is a furnace that produces heat continuously, day and night, and in winter it is busy losing that heat to the room around you. Against that constant production and loss, consider the drink: one mug, roughly 300ml of hot liquid, cooling inside you from drinking temperature down to body temperature. The total heat it can hand over on that journey is small — a minor entry in the body's daily energy accounts, gone within minutes. Your core temperature barely registers the event, and it does not need to: the drink was never going to be a measurable source of warmth for a whole body.

This is why the comparison at the end of this guide is so one-sided. Clothing and bedding work on the big term in the equation — the heat you are losing — for as long as you wear them. A drink works on the small term — a little heat added — for a few minutes. Both are worth having. They are not the same category of thing.

Why you feel warmer than the thermometer says

If the heat is negligible, why does a hot drink feel like such a powerful warmer? Three reasons, all real. First, your mouth and throat are full of temperature receptors, and warm liquid passing over them sends a strong "warming up" signal to the brain — the same system that makes a cold drink feel disproportionately refreshing on a hot day. Perception of warmth is not read from your core; it is assembled from signals like these, and the mouth shouts louder than most.

Second, your hands. Cold hands are one of the strongest contributors to feeling cold all over, because the body restricts blood flow to the fingers in the cold and they genuinely get chilly. Wrapping both hands around a hot mug warms them by direct conduction — real heat, really transferred, exactly where the discomfort is. Warm hands make the whole body feel warmer out of all proportion to the heat involved. Third, the pause: making a drink means standing in the warm kitchen, moving a little, then sitting down somewhere chosen, holding something hot, doing nothing for ten minutes. Rest, shelter and ritual are comforts in themselves, and comfort and warmth are close cousins in how the brain reports them. None of this is fake. It is simply perception and local warming doing the work, while the core temperature minds its own business.

Do hot drinks really keep you warm in winter for long?

No — the honest duration is minutes, not the evening. And there is a small catch at the scalding end of the scale. A very hot drink is a strong warming signal to the body's thermostat, and one response available to that thermostat is a light sweat: the body's way of shedding heat it believes it has in surplus. Indoors, bundled in winter layers, that can mean feeling faintly damp and then slightly cooler a little while later. The fix is simple and also better for the drink itself: comfortably hot, not throat-burning. Tea you can sip immediately beats tea you have to wait ten minutes to touch, for flavour and for physics.

The alcohol myth: feeling warm while getting colder

Now the important correction, because this myth is genuinely dangerous. Alcohol does not warm you up. It does something that feels identical and works in reverse. Alcohol widens the blood vessels near the skin — vasodilation — which sends a rush of warm blood to the surface. The skin flushes, the receptors in the skin report warmth, and you feel glowingly warm. Meanwhile, that warm blood is now at the surface, in contact with cold air, losing heat faster than it would have in the core — so core temperature can fall, faster, while the drinker feels warmer and takes fewer precautions. Feeling warm and being warm have parted company, which is precisely the hazardous combination in serious cold.

So the winter-night rule on this site is simple: the drink in the mug should be a drink that contains no alcohol if warmth is the point — and alcohol should never be anyone's strategy for staying warm outdoors in winter. The good news is that nothing is lost. Everything a hot drink actually does for you — the mouth-warming signal, the hot mug in the hands, the ritual pause — works exactly as well with hot chocolate, tea or spiced milk as it ever did with a toddy. Our recipe for café-style hot chocolate at home and our ginger tea for winter nights are both built on exactly that basis: all of the comfort, none of the false warmth.

What actually keeps you warm: the honest ranking

Since this site exists to keep people warm for less, here is the ranking the physiology supports. First, insulation on the body — layers, socks, a hat indoors if you are sitting still in a cold house — because slowing heat loss is the biggest lever you have. Second, warming the person rather than the room — a hot water bottle, a heated throw, an electric blanket pre-heating the bed; our comparison of the cheapest way to heat a room at night works through the costs of each. Third, movement — your furnace burns hotter when you use it, and ten minutes of tidying beats an hour of shivering elegantly on the sofa. Fourth, the warm room itself, for the hours you choose to heat it. And last, the hot drink — the smallest warmer on the list and, per unit of anything, quite possibly the most enjoyable. Make it because it is lovely. Just know what it is doing.

Frequently asked questions

Do hot drinks really keep you warm in winter?

They make you feel warmer much more than they make you warmer. A hot drink adds only a small amount of heat to a body that is constantly producing and losing far more, so the change in core temperature is tiny and brief. The comfort is real, though: warm receptors in the mouth, warm hands around the mug and the pause itself all signal warmth to the brain.

Does alcohol warm you up on a cold night?

No — it does the opposite while feeling like it helps. Alcohol widens the blood vessels near the skin, which sends warm blood to the surface, flushes the skin and creates a strong feeling of warmth. But that blood is at the surface losing heat to the cold air, so core temperature can fall faster. Feeling warm and being warm part company, which is exactly what makes alcohol risky in serious cold.

Why do my hands feel warmer after holding a hot mug?

Because that part is direct heat transfer, and it genuinely works. Cold hands are one of the strongest signals of feeling cold overall, and wrapping them around a hot mug warms the skin and the blood in your fingers by simple conduction. It is local rather than whole-body warming, but local is where the discomfort was.

Can a very hot drink make you feel colder afterwards?

It can, slightly. A very hot drink is also a warming signal to the body's thermostat, and one of the responses available to that thermostat is a light sweat — the body's way of shedding heat it thinks it has too much of. Indoors in winter clothes, that can leave you faintly damp and cooler a little later. Comfortably hot, rather than scalding, avoids most of this.

What warms you up more, a hot drink or another layer of clothing?

The layer, and it is not close. Clothing works for as long as you wear it by slowing the heat your body is constantly losing, which is a far bigger term in the equation than the small amount of heat a drink adds. Make the drink anyway — it warms your hands, your mouth and your mood — but put the jumper on first.
