How Many Blankets Do I Need in Winter?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only, not medical advice. Layer counts below are a practical starting point for adults — adjust to your own room, bedding and comfort.

If you keep asking how many blankets do I need in winter, the honest short answer is that there is no magic number. For most adults, the duvet counts as the main layer — usually the warmest thing on the bed — and you then add one or two additional layers depending on how warm or cold your bedroom actually runs. A warm, heated bedroom might need nothing extra at all. A cold, draughty one might need two. The right stack is the one you can adjust, not the tallest pile you can build.

A fixed number would mislead you, because bedrooms, duvets and pyjamas all differ — and two people in the same bed often differ as well. This guide gives you a practical way to count layers, match them to your room, and change them night to night instead of keeping one stack all season.

How many blankets do I need in winter? Start with the duvet

Count from the duvet outwards. Your duvet is layer one, and it provides most of your insulation. Anything on top of it — a blanket, a throw, a second light cover — is an extra layer you add for a reason: a colder room, a colder week, or a colder sleeper. Anything underneath, such as a top sheet, is a lighter layer that adds far less warmth.

So when someone says they sleep under “three blankets”, ask what those layers actually are. A sheet, a thin blanket and a light summer duvet is a very different bed from a winter duvet with two heavy blankets on top. Count warmth and adjustability instead: one main layer, plus extras you can add, remove or fold back without remaking the bed.

Room temperature is the other half of the equation. If you are not sure how cool your bedroom should be, our guide to the best bedroom temperature for sleep in winter explains the commonly cited range and how to measure your room at pillow height.

Warm, heated bedroomComfortable in light pyjamas, with no chillDuvet alone, or duvet plus one light blanket you can fold back
Cool bedroomA noticeable chill in the air, but no draughts across the bedDuvet plus one blanket on top
Cold, draughty bedroomCold air moving around windows or doors; bed feels icy at firstDuvet plus one blanket, with a second light layer to hand for the coldest nights

Notice that the chart stops adding layers at a point. Past that point, the problem is the room, not the bed: draughts strip warmth away no matter how much you pile on, and an icy bed is often better fixed by sealing gaps, pre-warming the bed, or warming your feet than by a third heavy cover. What actually counts as a layer?

People argue about blanket counts partly because they count different things. Here is a simple way to think about each piece on the bed:

  • Top sheet: a light layer. It adds a little insulation, keeps the duvet cleaner for longer, and lets you adjust warmth in small steps.
  • Duvet: your main layer. This is where most of the warmth comes from, and changing its warmth level changes the bed more than any other single swap.
  • Blanket: a true extra layer on top of the duvet, and your main adjustment tool — on for cold nights, folded back or off entirely on milder ones.
  • Throw: a smaller, lighter extra, useful across the feet or shoulders, where people often feel the cold first.
  • Pyjamas and bed socks: not bedding, but they change how warm you feel under the same stack.

How you arrange those layers matters as much as how many you use. Our guide to how to layer blankets on a bed for maximum warmth covers the order to put them in, and two thin blankets or one thick duvet: which is warmer? compares the flexibility of each approach.

Adjust night to night, not once a season

Winter is not one long, even cold spell. A mild, rainy week and a clear, frosty week call for different beds, and even within one night the room often cools towards the early hours. A fixed stack chosen in the coldest week will leave you overheating for much of the season.

Build adjustability in instead. Keep your main extra blanket folded at the foot of the bed, where you can pull it up half-asleep. Let each layer be removable on its own, rather than tucking everything in so tightly that changing anything means remaking the bed. And judge changes over a few nights, not one: a pattern of waking hot, or waking curled up and chilled, tells you which direction to move.

The sheet underneath plays a part too: whether flannel sheets are warmer than cotton sheets explains how much difference that layer really makes compared with the covers on top.

Keep a spare layer for the coldest weeks — and for guests

Whatever your normal stack is, keep one clean spare blanket per bed outside the everyday setup. It earns its place twice: during the coldest snap of the year, when even a cold-room stack may need help for a few nights, and when guests stay, because a visitor has no idea how your house runs at night and will usually guess wrong.

Store it where it can be reached in the dark — folded on a chair or in a drawer under the bed. A spare that lives in the loft is, in practice, no spare at all. And when the cold snap passes, take the extra layer back off: letting a temporary fix become the permanent stack is how beds drift into the too-many-layers problems below.

When partners need different amounts

Two people sharing a bed rarely share a thermostat. One sleeps hot, the other feels every draught, and a single shared stack forces one of them to lose.

Solve it on each side of the bed instead. Separate duvets are the simplest fix: each person keeps their own main layer and their own extras. A shared duvet with an extra throw on one side only also works. Even pyjamas can carry part of the difference, with one partner in lighter sleepwear under the same covers.

The downsides of too many layers

More is not always better. The first problem with an over-built bed is overheating: pile on enough insulation and you wake hot, sweaty and restless, kicking covers off and then waking again when you cool down. A bed that swings you between too hot and too cold is worse than a slightly cool bed that stays steady.

The second is weight: several heavy layers make turning over harder work and can leave the bed feeling like it is pinning you down. The third is practical — every extra blanket is another bulky item to wash, dry and store, and in winter, when drying space is limited, that laundry is a real cost. If your bed has crept up to four or five layers, try removing one for a few nights. Many people sleep no colder — just less encumbered.

Babies and young children: different rules entirely

Everything above is written for adults. Do not apply adult layer counts to a cot or a small child's bed, and do not add loose blankets based on how cold the room feels to you. Young children's bedding needs are different, and overheating is a particular concern for babies.

Instead, follow the current official safe-sleep guidance for your country, which covers room temperature, bedding and sleep position for each age, and check with your clinician, midwife or health visitor if anything is unclear. This is an area where guessing — or borrowing rules of thumb meant for grown-ups — is not good enough.

Frequently asked questions

How many blankets do I need in winter?

For most adults there is no single magic number. Count your duvet as the main insulating layer, then add layers by room temperature: often none or one extra blanket in a warm, heated bedroom, one in a cool bedroom, and one or two in a cold, draughty one. Adjust night to night rather than keeping one fixed stack all season.

Does the duvet count as a blanket layer?

Yes, for counting purposes the duvet is your main layer, and usually the warmest one. A top sheet is a light layer underneath it, a blanket or throw on top adds another, and pyjamas affect how warm you feel too. That is why two people with the same number of blankets can feel completely different levels of warmth.

How many layers should I use in a cold, draughty bedroom?

Start with your duvet plus one blanket, and keep a second light layer within reach for the coldest nights. Draughts matter as much as the layer count, because moving cold air keeps stripping warmth away, so sealing gaps and pre-warming the bed often help more than simply piling on a third heavy blanket.

Can too many blankets make me sleep worse?

Yes. Too many layers can leave you overheated and restless, add uncomfortable weight, trap sweat, and create a lot of extra washing and drying. If you regularly wake up hot, kick covers off, or find the bed heavy and hard to turn over in, remove a layer for a few nights and see whether you sleep better.

Do babies and young children need the same number of layers as adults?

No — do not apply adult layer counts to babies or young children. Their bedding and room temperature needs are different, and overheating is a particular concern. Follow the current official safe-sleep guidance for your country and your child's age, and ask your clinician, midwife or health visitor if you are unsure.
