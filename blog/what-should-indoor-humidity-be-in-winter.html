What Should Indoor Humidity Be in Winter? (It Depends on the Temperature Outside)

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — commonly cited comfort information about indoor air, not medical or housing advice. If discomfort or damp persists or worries you, see a professional.

If you are asking what should indoor humidity be in winter, you have probably already met the short answer — roughly 30–50% relative humidity — and found it does not quite settle the question. That range is worth knowing, and this guide uses it as the starting point. But it is commonly cited general guidance, not a rule, and one detail changes everything: the safe top of that range falls as the weather outside gets colder. A level that is comfortable in a mild spell can leave your windows streaming in a cold snap.

What should indoor humidity be in winter? The short answer

Relative humidity is the water vapour in the air expressed as a share of how much that air could hold at its current temperature. Warm air can hold more moisture than cold air. Heated winter air is the classic problem: cold outdoor air arrives holding little moisture, we warm it up, its capacity grows, but no water is added — so the relative share drops, and the air feels dry.

Against that background, roughly 30–50% relative humidity is the figure most commonly recommended for occupied homes in winter. Treat it exactly as that: a commonly cited, approximate comfort range from general published guidance — not a guarantee, not a building standard, and not a target to chase to the nearest percent. Homes differ in insulation, windows, ventilation and climate, and those differences matter more than false precision.

Hold the range as two boundaries. Below roughly 30%, many people start to notice dry-air discomfort. Above roughly 50%, condensation risk on cold surfaces climbs — and in cold weather, it climbs well before 50%.

Why the safe ceiling falls as it gets colder outside

Condensation is the referee here. Indoor air carries moisture from breathing, cooking, showering and laundry. When that moist air touches a surface colder than its dew point — the temperature at which vapour turns back into liquid water — droplets form. In winter, the coldest surfaces are usually window glass, frames, and poorly insulated corners of outside walls.

The colder it is outside, the colder those surfaces become. On a mild day, glass may stay warm enough that air at 45% never condenses on it. In a hard frost, that same glass is far colder, and the same 45% condenses readily. Nothing about your home changed; the ceiling simply moved.

Read this table as what it is: approximate, commonly cited guidance of the kind published for cold-climate housing, not a precise formula. Well-insulated homes with modern windows often tolerate the higher side of each band; older homes with single glazing may see condensation lower down. The real test is your windows: light misting on the coldest mornings is common, but water running down glass most days means the level is too high for the weather.

How to measure: the hygrometer habit

Feelings are a poor instrument: a cool room can feel damp at a humidity a warm room finds comfortable. A hygrometer — a small instrument that reads relative humidity — replaces guessing with a number. No particular model is needed; placement and habit matter more than the device.

Place it in a room you actually use, at roughly sitting or bedside height, away from radiators, direct sun, exterior doors and any humidifier, and leave it to settle after moving it. Then measure room by room: a bedroom with the door closed all night and a cold window can run quite differently from the hallway, often the most averaged, least informative spot in the house. The bedroom reading usually matters most, since that is where you spend the longest stretch breathing the same air.

Finally, read trends, not moments. Cooking and showers spike humidity briefly and harmlessly. Check at a similar time of day across several days, and note the outdoor temperature alongside.

Signs your air is too dry — or too humid

Signs the air may be too dry

Commonly noticed signs include waking with a parched mouth or scratchy-feeling throat, skin that feels tight, static shocks from clothing and bedding, and wooden furniture developing small gaps. These are general comfort observations only — many things can cause similar feelings, and this guide makes no medical claims. If symptoms persist or worry you, ask a professional. Let the hygrometer confirm dryness before you act.

Signs the air may be too humid

The clearest sign is condensation: windows misted or wet most mornings, water pooling on sills, or damp patches in cold corners, behind furniture on outside walls, or around window frames. A persistent musty smell and mould spots in those same places point the same way. Too-humid air is a building problem as much as a comfort one, because surfaces that stay damp can grow mould. If readings sit high and windows stream, the answer is less moisture and better ventilation — not more heat alone.

The condensation and mould trade-off, plainly

Every winter humidity decision is this trade-off. Drier air can feel uncomfortable. Wetter air feels softer — right up to the point where its moisture starts landing on your coldest surfaces as water. That water is the real cost of overshooting: it soaks into frames and plaster at the coldest, slowest-drying spots, and surfaces kept damp that way can support mould growth over time.

So the goal is not maximum humidity; it is the middle — high enough to feel comfortable, low enough that windows and cold corners stay dry in the weather you are actually having. In mild weather that middle sits higher; in a cold snap it sits lower. Let the target slide with the temperature.

Why this matters most in Canada and other cold climates

The colder and longer the winter, the sharper this trade-off becomes. In much of Canada, and in other regions with sustained sub-zero weather, temperatures can sit in the lowest bands of the table above for weeks. Indoor air is heated for months, so it trends very dry — while window and wall surfaces stay cold enough to punish any attempt to fix that dryness by humidifying aggressively.

Cold-climate homes therefore live in a narrower window: dry enough to protect the building, moist enough to feel bearable, with the balance shifting as each cold snap arrives and lifts. The habit that travels well everywhere: measure, watch the windows, adjust in small steps.

What to adjust first

Start with habits before hardware. Ventilation first: brief, deliberate airing — windows opened wide for a short spell, extractor fans used while cooking and showering and for a while afterwards — clears moist air without chilling the home. Humidifier output second: if you run one, its dial is your most direct control; turn it down at the first sign of regular window condensation, and remember the target itself should fall as temperatures fall. Laundry third: drying clothes indoors releases a lot of moisture, which helps a genuinely dry home and harms a damp-prone one — dry in one ventilated room, or ease off during cold snaps.

Change one thing at a time, then re-measure over a few days. Humidity responds slowly, and changing everything at once leaves you not knowing what worked. Small steps, checked against the hygrometer and the windows, will find your home's real middle more reliably than any single number in a guide.

Frequently asked questions

What should indoor humidity be in winter?

Commonly cited general guidance suggests roughly 30–50% relative humidity indoors in winter, presented as an approximate comfort range rather than a strict rule. In colder weather, aim nearer the lower end, because the safe ceiling falls as outdoor temperatures drop. Watch your windows: regular condensation is a sign the level is too high for the conditions outside.

Why does the right humidity depend on the temperature outside?

Because moist indoor air condenses when it meets a cold surface. The colder it is outside, the colder your window glass and exposed wall surfaces become, so condensation forms at a lower indoor humidity. That is why guidance that is fine in mild weather around 10°C can be too humid in deep cold around −20°C, even in the same home.

How do I measure indoor humidity accurately?

Use a hygrometer, placed in a lived-in room away from radiators, humidifiers, kitchens and steamy bathrooms, and leave it to settle before reading it. Check the bedroom separately from the hallway, because levels vary room to room. Compare readings at a similar time of day over several days rather than trusting a single number.

What are the signs my home is too dry or too humid in winter?

Commonly noticed signs of dry air include a parched feeling on waking, tight-feeling skin, static on clothing and very dry wooden furniture, though these are general comfort observations, not a diagnosis. Signs of air that is too humid include frequent window condensation, a musty smell, and damp patches or mould in cold corners. A hygrometer reading helps you confirm which problem you actually have.

What should I adjust first if my humidity is wrong?

Start with the free habits: ventilate briefly each day, use extractor fans or lids when cooking and showering, and rethink drying laundry indoors if windows are misting. If you run a humidifier, turn its output down before anything else when condensation appears. Change one thing at a time, then re-measure over a few days to see the effect.
