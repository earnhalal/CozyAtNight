How to Bleed a Radiator Cold at the Top (In About 10 Minutes)

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — not heating-engineering advice. If you are unsure about your boiler or system pressure, or the problem keeps returning, use a qualified heating engineer.

Learning how to bleed a radiator cold at the top is one of the highest-value ten minutes in home maintenance. The symptom is unmistakable: with the heating running, the radiator is hot at the bottom and cool or stone cold across the top. Hot water enters low and should fill the whole panel; where air has collected at the top, water cannot reach, and that part of the radiator simply does not heat. Bleeding means opening a small vent valve at the top corner to let the trapped air hiss out until water arrives — then the radiator fills and heats evenly again.

This guide covers how to recognise the problem, what you need, the careful step-by-step, and the boiler pressure check that finishes the job properly. It is one of the first checks in our room-by-room diagnosis of why a house stays cold even with the heating on.

Is it air? The two-minute diagnosis

Run the heating for twenty minutes, then feel the suspect radiator from bottom to top with the flat of your hand. Cold top and warm bottom: air, and bleeding is the fix. Cold bottom or cold patch in the middle with a warm top: that is more likely sludge settling, which bleeding will not cure — it needs an engineer's clean. Whole radiator cold while others are hot: check its valves are open first; a thermostatic valve pin can stick shut after summer, and a lockshield may have been closed. Only bleed when the pattern says air.

You may also hear the system telling you: gurgling, ticking or whooshing from a radiator is often air moving around inside it. And if you have recently had plumbing work, a drained system, or a radiator removed and refitted, air in the system afterwards is almost guaranteed.

What you need, and the safety setup

You need a radiator bleed key (a small square-socket key, sold everywhere hardware is sold; some modern valves accept a flat screwdriver), an old cloth or towel, and a small cup or jug. That is all.

The safety setup matters more than the tools. Turn the heating off and let the radiator cool before you start. Water in a running system can be hot enough to scald, and bleeding a running, pumped system can draw air in rather than push it out. Lay the towel under the vent corner to protect the floor — the water that eventually escapes can be dark and will stain carpet and wood. Keep the cup ready under the valve. If at any point you cannot identify the vent valve, or the valve looks corroded or damaged, stop and call an engineer instead.

How to bleed a radiator cold at the top, step by step

  1. Heating off, radiator cool. Confirm both. Work on one radiator at a time, starting with the one that feels coldest at the top — often the highest radiator in the house, since air rises.
  2. Find the vent valve. It is a small square or slotted nub at the top corner of the radiator, usually at the back edge. Fit the key or screwdriver onto it.
  3. Cloth in hand, cup underneath. Wrap the cloth around the valve area to catch drips, and hold the cup below.
  4. Open a quarter to half a turn, anticlockwise. You should hear a hiss — that is the trapped air escaping. Do not unscrew it fully; a small opening is all air needs.
  5. Wait for the hiss to stop. When the hissing fades and water begins to dribble steadily, the air is out and the radiator is full again.
  6. Close the valve firmly. Turn clockwise until snug — hand-tight with the key, not forced. Wipe the corner dry and check after a few minutes that it is not weeping.
  7. Repeat on other radiators that showed the cold-top pattern, then move to the pressure check below before switching the heating back on.

The step people skip: boiler pressure

Letting air out lets water settle, and on a sealed system the pressure can drop. After bleeding, check the pressure gauge on your boiler (or its display) against the manufacturer's recommended range — commonly around 1 to 1.5 bar when cold on many systems, but your manual is the authority, not this page. If pressure is low, top the system up via the filling loop exactly as the manual describes, or ask an engineer if you have never done it. Switch the heating on and re-check the bled radiators after twenty minutes: the whole panel should now be warm, top included.

If the cold top keeps coming back

Needing to bleed once at the start of the season is normal. Needing to bleed the same radiator every few weeks is a symptom, not a routine: air is entering somewhere, or corrosion inside the system is generating gas — both are engineer territory, because they point at leaks, failing components or water chemistry that shortens the system's life. Likewise call a professional if a valve will not close, weeps after tightening, or if pressure keeps falling — and never open the boiler casing yourself. For the wider picture — valves, balancing, thermostats and draughts — our guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up picks up where bleeding leaves off.

Which radiator do you bleed first?

If several radiators show the cold-top pattern, start with the one on the highest floor and furthest into the house: air rises and collects at the top of the system, and the highest radiators usually hold the most. Work down through the rest that need it, then do the pressure check once, at the end, rather than between radiators. Keep a note of which radiators needed air — if the same one appears on the list every few weeks, that repetition is the clue your engineer needs, pointing at where air or gas is entering the system.

Make bleeding part of an autumn routine, alongside checking the boiler's service date and testing radiator valves that have sat still all summer. Ten minutes in October, with the system cool and no pressure of a cold house that evening, beats the same job discovered in January with guests arriving. And if a thermostatic valve has stuck shut over summer — the radiator stays cold everywhere while its pipes are hot — that is a valve problem, not an air problem; the pin under the valve head sometimes frees with gentle pressure, but force nothing, and call an engineer if it will not move.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if a radiator needs bleeding?

With the heating running, feel the radiator: cold at the top and warm at the bottom means trapped air is occupying the top of the panel, and bleeding is the fix. Gurgling or ticking noises from a radiator are another common sign. Cold at the bottom suggests sludge instead, which bleeding cannot cure.

Should the heating be on or off when bleeding a radiator?

Off — and let the radiator cool first. Water in a running system can be hot enough to scald, and a running pump can pull air in rather than push it out. Bleed with the system off and cool, then check the boiler pressure before switching back on.

What happens after I bleed a radiator?

Air hisses out of the vent valve; when the hiss stops and water dribbles steadily, close the valve. Then check the boiler pressure gauge — bleeding can drop pressure on a sealed system — and top up via the filling loop only as your boiler manual describes. Re-check the radiator once the heating runs again.

Why does my radiator keep going cold at the top?

If the same radiator needs bleeding every few weeks, air or gas is entering the system somewhere — a small leak, a failing component, or corrosion generating gas inside. That pattern is a symptom to have investigated by a heating engineer, not a routine to repeat all winter.

What if I do not have a radiator bleed key?

Bleed keys are small, inexpensive and sold in any hardware shop; some modern vent valves accept a flat-head screwdriver instead. Never force a valve with pliers or the wrong tool — a damaged valve that will not close turns a ten-minute job into a leak, and is a reason to call an engineer.
