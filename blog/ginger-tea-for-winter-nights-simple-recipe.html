Ginger Tea for Winter Nights: A Simple Recipe With Lemon and Honey

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only — this is a recipe for a warming drink, not a health remedy or medical advice.

Call it a ginger tea for winter nights simple recipe, because that is exactly what it is: fresh ginger, water, lemon and honey, simmered while the evening closes in. There is no special equipment, no rare ingredient and nothing to order in. What there is, is a drink with a genuine, peppery warmth that builds as you sip — the kind of cup you wrap both hands around — and a kitchen that smells like somebody competent and kind lives there. It takes fifteen minutes, most of them unattended.

A quick word on what this recipe is not. You will find no health claims on this page. Ginger tea is presented here the way we think it should be: as a delicious, warming, caffeine-free drink for cold evenings. If you are looking for a remedy for something, that search belongs with a professional — this page is a recipe, honestly labelled.

Ingredients: two generous mugs

  • A thumb-sized piece of fresh ginger, roughly 25–30g — more if you like it fiery
  • 600ml (about 2½ cups) water
  • Juice of half a lemon, plus an extra slice or two to serve
  • 1–2 teaspoons honey, to taste
  • Optional extras, any one of them: a cinnamon stick, two or three whole cloves, a strip of orange peel, or a few black peppercorns for extra fire

On the ginger: fresh is the recipe. Ground ginger powder makes a different, duller drink and settles into sediment; a firm fresh root, heavy for its size with smooth, tight skin, is what you want. There is no need to peel it with any ceremony — a good wash and a scrape with a teaspoon takes the skin off in seconds, and for a rustic cup you can simply slice it unpeeled and strain well.

The ginger tea for winter nights simple recipe, step by step

  1. Slice the ginger thinly. Thin slices or rough matchsticks give the water the most surface to work on. The finer you cut, the stronger the tea for the same simmering time.
  2. Put the ginger and water in a small pan and bring to the boil over a medium heat.
  3. Simmer gently for 10 to 15 minutes. A lazy bubble, not a rolling boil. Add the cinnamon stick or cloves now if you are using them. The kitchen will smell wonderful; the tea is turning golden.
  4. Rest it off the heat for two minutes. This small pause settles the fiercest edge and lets the flavour round out.
  5. Strain into mugs. A small sieve or tea strainer catches the slices. Press the ginger lightly with a spoon to release the last of the juice.
  6. Add lemon and honey now — not during the simmer. Lemon boiled tastes flat and bitter at the edges; lemon added fresh stays bright. Honey stirred into tea that has cooled for a minute keeps more of its delicate flavour than honey boiled in the pan.
  7. Taste and adjust. More lemon for sharpness, more honey for softness, a splash of hot water if you overshot the fire. Serve with the extra lemon slice if you like things pretty.

Adjusting heat, strength and sweetness

The recipe above is a middle path, and ginger is forgiving — three levers put the cup exactly where you want it. Time is the strength lever: five minutes of simmering gives a mild, fragrant tea; twenty gives a drink with real fire at the back of the throat. Cut is the efficiency lever: grated ginger releases more flavour faster than slices, so a grated batch needs less time — and a thorough straining. The lemon-honey balance is the character lever: lemon forward tastes bright and almost savoury; honey forward tastes soft and round. Make it twice, changing one thing each time, and you will have your house version for the rest of the winter.

An honest word about health claims

Ginger has been a treasured ingredient in cooking traditions for a very long time, and ginger tea is surrounded by a fog of claims — that it cures this, prevents that, fixes the other. This site does not sell drinks as medicine, so here is our complete position: ginger tea is a hot, delicious drink; hot drinks are comforting; comfort on a winter night is a good in itself. That is enough reason to make it. As with any food or drink, if you are pregnant, taking medication or managing a health condition and you are unsure about an ingredient, check with a professional who knows your situation. Everyone else: put the kettle on.

Make it ahead, and make it a ritual

Ginger tea batches well. Double or triple the recipe, cool it, and keep it covered in the fridge for two to three days; reheat gently by the mugful and add lemon and honey fresh at serving time if you can, because both taste brighter added late. On busy weeks, a jug of it in the fridge is the difference between the ritual happening and not happening. And the ritual is the point: this is the drink to carry to the sofa or the bed in the last hour of the evening, ideally alongside the rest of a wind-down — dimmed lights, a warm bed waiting, screens going dark. It pairs with our guide to whether hot drinks really keep you warm, which explains honestly what the mug is and is not doing, and on richer evenings it alternates with our café-style hot chocolate. Under a heated throw, with the house cooling down around you, it is hard to do better for the price of a piece of ginger.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I simmer fresh ginger tea?

Simmer the sliced ginger gently for about 10 to 15 minutes for a balanced cup. Five minutes makes a mild, fragrant tea; twenty minutes makes a fiercely spicy one. Taste as you go the first few times and note your own number — after that, the recipe is yours rather than ours.

Can I make ginger tea without honey?

Yes. Honey is there for sweetness and softness, not structure. Leave it out for a sharper, more savoury cup, or replace it with sugar, maple syrup or any sweetener you already use. The lemon does more of the balancing work than the honey does, so keep the lemon in.

Does ginger tea contain caffeine?

No. Despite the name, this is an infusion of ginger root in hot water — there is no tea leaf in it at all, and therefore no caffeine. That is a large part of why it suits winter evenings: it gives you the hot-drink ritual close to bedtime without the stimulant that can disturb sleep.

Can I make a bigger batch of ginger tea and reheat it?

Yes. Make a double or triple batch, let it cool, and keep it in a covered jug in the fridge for two to three days. Reheat it gently in a pan or mug until steaming, and add the lemon and honey fresh to each mug if you can — they taste brighter added at serving time than after days in the fridge.

Is ginger tea a remedy for colds?

We make no such claim, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does on a recipe page. Ginger tea is a warming, comforting drink, and a hot drink can feel wonderful when you are under the weather — but feeling comforted is not a cure. If you are ill, rest, fluids and proper medical advice are the honest prescription.
