How to Draught-Proof Sash Windows Yourself (Renter-Friendly Options Included)

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. On listed buildings or in conservation areas, check what is permitted before altering original windows.

Learning how to draught proof sash windows yourself starts with understanding why they leak: a sash window is designed to slide, so it is built with small clearances — and every clearance is a path for cold air. Air comes in where the sashes meet in the middle (the meeting rails), along the vertical sides where the sashes run against the parting beads, at the bottom of the lower sash against the sill, and around the outside of the frame where it meets the wall. The good news is that each leak has a known, DIY-able fix, and the best of them — brush pile seals — were designed for exactly this job and last for years.

This guide goes leak by leak, in the order most people should tackle them, and marks clearly which fixes are reversible for renters. It follows the same find-first discipline as our guide to how to find draughts in your home: two minutes with a tissue will tell you which of the four leaks your windows actually have.

How to draught proof sash windows yourself: start with the meeting rails

The horizontal joint where the two sashes overlap is usually the biggest leak, because it is a long, straight gap across the full width of the window. The proper fix is a brush pile strip routed or pinned into the meeting rail of the upper sash, so the brushes press against the lower sash when the window closes. Brush-pile carrier strips that screw or pin on exist for DIY fitting without routing. The window must still close and latch fully — the fastener pulling the rails together is part of the seal — so check the latch pulls them tight, and adjust or replace a loose fastener before blaming the seals.

The sides: parting beads and staff beads

Down each side, the sashes run in channels formed by thin wooden strips: the parting bead between the two sashes and the staff bead on the room side. Replacement beads with brush pile already fitted are the standard renovation answer — the old bead is levered out, the new one pinned in its place, and the sash then slides against a continuous brush instead of a rattling wooden gap. This is the most involved of the DIY fixes, because the sashes may need to come out, and sash cords and weights live behind those beads. If your windows rattle and stick, this is also the moment to check cords before one fails. Confident DIYers do this job routinely; if the window is large, painted shut in layers, or the cords are frayed, a sash specialist earns their fee.

The bottom rail, the sill and the frame edges

Where the bottom of the lower sash meets the sill, a brush or compression strip on the underside of the rail closes the third gap. And where the whole frame meets the surrounding wall, cracked paint and shrunken filler leave a fourth leak that belongs to the wall, not the window: rake out failed material and reseal with a suitable flexible filler or sealant. While you are there, check the sill itself for rot — a soft sill will never seal, and catching decay early is far cheaper than replacing the frame later. These perimeter leaks are covered, with the rest of the house's, in our draught fixes under $25.

Renter-friendly and reversible options

Renting, or not ready for the full bead replacement? These help without altering the window. Self-adhesive foam or V-strip tape applied to the meeting rail and frame stops much of the air for a season or two — cheap, quick, and removable, though it wears and can peel paint if ripped off carelessly; warm it and peel slowly at the end. Shrink-film secondary glazing — a clear film taped over the whole window and tightened with warm air — seals every leak at once and adds an insulating air layer; our honest assessment is in does window insulation film work. The trade-off: the window cannot open while the film is on, so keep it for windows you leave shut all winter, and never block a window you rely on for ventilation or escape. Heavy curtains, closed at dusk on a rail that lets them fall close to the wall, blunt what leaks remain.

What not to do

Do not seal a sash window permanently shut with sealant or paint unless you are certain it is never needed for ventilation or as an escape route — and even then, think twice; a sealed-shut window is a problem you hand to the next owner. Do not block trickle vents or air bricks while draught-proofing: homes need deliberate ventilation, and the line between draught-proofing and suffocating a damp house is covered in our guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up. And on a listed building or in a conservation area, check the rules before replacing beads or adding anything visible — original windows are often protected features.

Tools, materials and a realistic afternoon

For the tape-and-film tier you need almost nothing: scissors, a tape measure, a hairdryer for film, and clean cloths so adhesives grip. For brush work, add a pin punch or small screws for carrier strips, a junior hacksaw to cut strips to length, and a levering tool for old beads — plus patience with paint that has glued beads in place over decades. Budget a realistic afternoon per window for bead work the first time; the second window goes twice as fast. Work top-down (meeting rail, then sides, then sill) so any strip you fit is not disturbed by the next job.

Know when to stop and call a sash specialist: rotten sills or rails, broken sash cords with weights dropped inside the box, cracked panes, or windows that are structurally loose in their frame are joinery, not draught-proofing. A specialist can often renovate and draught-proof in one visit, reusing the original sashes — which matters for period homes, where the windows are part of the building's character and, in many areas, its protected fabric. Photograph each window before you start; if a bead run goes wrong halfway, a photo of the original arrangement is worth more than any diagram, including the ones in this guide — and the remaining leaks will still show up honestly in the tissue test from our draught fixes checklist afterwards.

Keep the removed beads and any original hardware you replace, labelled by window — future owners and joiners will thank you, and original parts keep a period window reversible.

Frequently asked questions

Where do sash windows leak the most?

Usually at the meeting rails — the long horizontal joint where the two sashes overlap — followed by the side channels against the parting beads, the bottom rail against the sill, and the frame's outer edge against the wall. A tissue test on a windy day will show you which of the four your windows have.

Can I draught-proof sash windows without stopping them sliding?

Yes. Brush pile seals are designed for sliding windows: fitted into the meeting rail and replacement parting and staff beads, they compress as the sash passes and seal when it rests. The window should still open, close and latch fully after fitting — check that before you finish.

What is the best renter-friendly fix for draughty sash windows?

Shrink-film secondary glazing seals every leak at once and removes cleanly, though the window cannot open while it is fitted. Self-adhesive foam or V-strip on the meeting rail is the other reversible option. Heavy curtains closed at dusk blunt whatever draught remains.

How long do brush pile seals last?

Properly fitted brush pile in beads and meeting rails lasts for years — it is the standard renovation approach, unlike foam tape, which typically gives a season or two before compressing and peeling. That durability is why the bead work is worth doing once, well.

Should I seal my sash windows completely shut?

No. Never seal shut a window you need for ventilation or escape, and think carefully even about the others — moisture needs a way out of every home. Draught-proof the gaps around and between the sashes instead, and leave purpose-made ventilation alone.
