Do Thermal Curtains Really Work? What They Can and Cannot Do

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Keep curtains clear of radiators and any open flame, and never cover vents or heaters with fabric.

Do thermal curtains really work? Yes — with a condition that decides everything: they work when they are fitted like a seal, not hung like a decoration. A thermal curtain is a lined, heavier curtain that, closed against a window, traps a layer of still air between fabric and glass. Still air insulates — the same principle behind the film in our guide does window insulation film work — so the curtain slows heat loss through the glass and stops the cold air that forms at the pane from cascading into the room. What it cannot do is seal a leaky frame: air that comes around the window will simply come around the curtain too.

This guide covers what thermal curtains genuinely achieve, the fitting details that separate a working pair from an expensive pair, and the honest limits — including the radiator mistake that wastes more heat than curtains save.

What thermal curtains actually do

Two effects, both real. First, insulation at the glass: single glazing especially sheds heat, and the still air layer behind a closed, well-fitted curtain slows that loss for the hours the curtain is shut — which happen to be the coldest hours, the evening and night. Second, comfort: air cooled by the glass normally sinks and spills across the floor as a slow, cold draught you feel on your ankles. A curtain that reaches the sill or floor interrupts that cascade, and rooms commonly feel warmer at the same thermostat setting — the same surface-and-movement effect behind much of why a house feels cold even with the heating on.

Set expectations by the window, though. On single glazing, the improvement is noticeable. On modern double glazing in good repair, the glass was losing far less, so curtains add comfort more than savings. No curtain fixes a frame leak — for that you need the seals and strips in draught fixes under $25 first, and curtains second.

Do thermal curtains really work? Only if they fit like this

Length: to the sill at minimum, to the floor or just above it ideally, so cold air cannot spill out at the bottom. Width: generous — enough fabric to fall in folds and still overlap the wall at both sides, because the edges are where the air layer leaks. The top: the forgotten gap. Warm room air flows in over the top of the rail, gets cooled behind the curtain and pours out at the bottom — a slow loop that ruins the effect. A pelmet, a valance, or a rail fitted close to the wall blocks that loop; even the curtain's own header, hung tight to the wall, helps. The middle: pairs should overlap where they meet, not just touch. Fit like this, a curtain is a seal; hung narrow and short, it is a scarf on a window.

The radiator mistake — and the moisture question

If a radiator sits under the window, a long curtain tucked behind it sends the radiator's heat straight up behind the fabric and out through the glass — the worst arrangement in the house. Either lift the curtains onto the sill above the radiator, or let them fall in front of it (accepting they will billow) — never behind. Better still, close curtains at dusk but keep them clear of the radiator's rising air, as covered in our guide to keeping a house warm without turning the heating up.

Moisture is the other honest caveat. The air trapped behind a curtain is cold and can condense on the glass, out of sight. On windows prone to condensation, check behind the curtains in the morning, wipe the glass, and let air circulate during the day with curtains open — the routine in our condensation guide applies. A curtain that hides a mouldy window frame all winter has cost more than it saved.

The verdict

Thermal curtains are a real measure with a fitting condition. Generous, floor-length, overlapped, with the top gap blocked, closed from dusk — on single-glazed or draughty-feeling windows they earn their place, and in bedrooms they work the longest hours. Bought short and narrow for looks, or tucked behind radiators, they are decoration wearing a technical name. Measure first, hang them like a seal, and open them in the morning to let the sun and air undo the night's moisture.

Choosing curtains that can actually do the job

Without naming brands — the principles matter more — look for three things. Weight and lining: a curtain with a separate, dense lining traps air far better than a single layer of fashion fabric; hold it to the light and a tight weave shows less glow. Coverage you can calculate: measure the window, then buy for the floor, the overlap and the folds — a curtain stretched flat to just cover the glass has nothing left to seal with. A header that sits close: tracks and rails mounted tight to the wall, or a pelmet above, starve the cold loop at the top. Eyelets on a pole look clean but hold the fabric away from the wall at exactly the wrong place; if that is your fitting, a pelmet board above it does the sealing work instead.

Finally, compare honestly with the alternatives before spending. Film, assessed in does window insulation film work, seals harder but locks the window. Secondary glazing seals hardest of all and costs the most. Curtains sit in the flexible middle: daily open-and-close, decent insulation, no installation beyond the rail. For the windows you live beside — the sofa window, the bed window — that flexibility is usually what makes them, genuinely, really work.

One small habit multiplies whatever your curtains can do: open them fully on sunny winter mornings. Sun through glass is free heat, and a curtain left closed against a bright window refuses it. Dusk-close, dawn-open is the rhythm that makes curtains a system rather than a purchase — and it costs nothing at all.

And measure the room, not just the window, before you buy: the coldest glass usually belongs to the room you sit in longest, so that is where the best curtains belong. Moving a good pair from a spare room to the living room for the winter is a legitimate, zero-cost version of this entire guide.

Frequently asked questions

Do thermal curtains really work, or is it marketing?

They work by a real mechanism: closed, well-fitted curtains trap a layer of still air against the glass, and still air insulates. The effect is most noticeable on single glazing and during the evening and night hours when curtains are shut. What is marketing is any curtain working regardless of fit — length, overlap and the gap at the top decide the result.

What length should thermal curtains be?

To the floor, or just above it, is best, so cooled air cannot spill out at the bottom; sill length is the minimum. They should also be wide enough to overlap the wall at both sides and overlap each other in the middle, with the gap above the rail blocked by a pelmet or a close-fitting rail.

Should thermal curtains go in front of or behind a radiator?

Never behind. A curtain tucked behind a radiator under the window channels the radiator's heat up and out through the glass. Either lift the curtains to sill length above the radiator, or let them hang in front of it — and keep fabric clear of the heat as the curtain and radiator makers advise.

Do thermal curtains cause condensation?

They can hide it rather than cause it: the still air behind the curtain is cold, so moisture condenses on the glass out of sight. Check and wipe the glass in the morning, open curtains during the day to air the window, and deal with persistent condensation using a ventilation routine.

Are thermal curtains better than window insulation film?

They solve overlapping problems differently. Film seals the window itself — better against frame draughts — but the window cannot open. Curtains can be opened daily and suit windows you still use, but they cannot seal frame leaks. Many homes use film on shut windows and good curtains on the rest.
