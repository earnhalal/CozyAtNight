Is It Cheaper to Leave Heating On Low All Day or Turn It On and Off? The Honest Answer

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Energy prices vary by country, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

Every winter the same debate returns: is it cheaper to leave heating on low all day or turn it on and off when you need it? For most UK homes with a boiler and radiators, the honest answer is to turn it on and off — heat only when you are there to feel it. Leaving it on low feels thrifty, but usually means paying to replace heat nobody was home to enjoy.

Below: how heat loss works, why the myth is so believable, the exceptions where steady low heat makes sense, and a timer strategy that works either way. It sits alongside our guide to the cheapest way to heat a room at night, compared room by room, where the same principle — warm people, not empty hours — does most of the saving.

Is it cheaper to leave heating on low all day or turn it on and off? The short answer

For a home that is empty for part of the day, or where everyone sleeps under warm bedding at night, heating in timed bursts costs less than holding a low temperature around the clock. The reason is not boiler efficiency at different settings — it is simpler, and it is about your walls, windows and roof.

Your home loses heat continuously whenever it is warmer inside than outside — through walls, windows, roof, floor and every draught — and the warmer you keep it, the faster it escapes, because a bigger temperature gap pushes heat through harder. Keeping a house warm is not a one-off purchase; it is a standing charge, paid for every warm hour.

Hold a home warm for 24 hours and you pay for 24 hours of heat loss. Let it cool while you are out and you pay for fewer hours — and cooling hours lose heat ever more slowly. The reheat does not cancel that saving.

A simple worked illustration — and the cost formula

No statistics are needed, just a labelled illustration. Imagine two identical days in the same house. On day one, heating holds the house warm from 6am to 10pm — sixteen hours of replacing lost heat. On day two, it holds the same temperature for twenty-four hours: eight extra warm hours, each leaking heat the boiler must replace. Eight extra hours of loss cannot cost the same as none. That is the whole argument, whatever the tariff.

Where money comes in, use the formula from every running-cost guide on this site: cost per hour = kilowatts × price per kWh. For direct electric heating that is literal: a 2 kW heater at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh costs 2 × 24p = 48p per hour; at an illustrative US rate of 16¢ per kWh, 32¢ per hour.

Illustrative rates, clearly labelled. This article uses 24p per kWh as an illustrative UK rate and 16 US cents per kWh as an illustrative US rate. Most UK central heating runs on gas, also priced per kWh at a different rate — check your own bill. The principle is identical for any fuel: the longer the system holds a temperature, the more units you buy.

For a boiler system, read the formula as energy: every kilowatt-hour of heat the house loses is a kilowatt-hour the boiler must supply, and you are billed for the fuel behind it. Longer warm hours mean more kilowatt-hours lost, and more bought.

Why the myth persists: reheating feels expensive

If the maths is this clear, why do so many sensible people believe the opposite? Because your ears seem to point the other way. Come home to a cold house and the boiler fires hard while radiators warm up — it feels like guzzling energy. Left on low, the boiler only ticks over quietly. Ticking over feels cheap; roaring feels expensive.

Feelings measure the wrong thing. What matters is how many kilowatt-hours the boiler supplies across the day, not how hard it works in ten minutes. The busy reheat only replaces heat lost during the off period — when the cooler house was losing heat more slowly. That total is smaller than what would have leaked away had the house stayed warm. Short and busy beats long and gentle, because long and gentle never stops paying the standing charge.

The myth also holds a grain of comfort truth: a house kept on low never feels cold when you walk in. That comfort is real — it is simply not free, and should be chosen as comfort, not believed as a saving.

The honest exceptions: when steady low heat does make sense

Myth-busting that ignores the exceptions is just another myth, so here they are.

Homes occupied all day. If someone is home and awake all day, heating is needed most of the day anyway. The debate shrinks to overnight hours and short trips out, and a steady setting with a modest night setback is reasonable.

Heat pumps work differently. A heat pump generally works best running steadily at a lower flow temperature, not in short hot bursts, so boiler timing advice should not be copied across. Follow your own system's guidance, which usually favours gentle, continuous running with small setbacks.

Very cold weather, pipes and damp. In a hard cold snap, or where pipes run through cold spaces, letting temperature fall too far risks frozen and burst pipes, and cold surfaces where condensation feeds damp and mould. A low background level as protection is not waste — it is insurance. The same applies to an empty house: frost protection first, savings second.

Very poorly insulated homes need extra care. Temperatures fall fast when heating stops, and rooms can sit cold and damp. Fewer warm hours still cost less, but the practical answer may be shorter off-periods and a higher minimum while draught-proofing and insulation are tackled — see our guide to how to keep your house warm without turning the heating up.

A timer and thermostat strategy that actually saves

Most systems already have a timer and a thermostat; the saving is in how they are set.

  • Time around real life. Set heating to come on shortly before you get up and before you return home, and to go off when you leave and a little before bed. Adjust the lead time by experiment.
  • Let the thermostat do the stopping. The timer decides when heat is available; the thermostat decides when it is needed. Set it to the lowest comfortable temperature, and let it switch the system off on milder days even inside a timed period.
  • Use a night setback, not a deep freeze. Overnight, bedding does the warming, so the house can sit cooler — but keep a sensible minimum in cold weather. Never let a home drop so low that pipes freeze or damp and mould grow; if in doubt, raise the minimum.
  • Zone by room. Radiator valves let you heat occupied rooms properly and keep spare rooms and hallways cooler. Heating fewer rooms is the quiet twin of heating for fewer hours. Close doors to keep warmth where you paid for it.
  • Review after a week. Adjust timings in small steps rather than abandoning the schedule after one chilly morning.

Safety, briefly: keep boiler servicing up to date, never block ventilation a heating appliance needs, and if you use a portable heater alongside central heating, plug it directly into a wall socket, keep it clear of bedding and curtains, and never cover it.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to leave heating on low all day or turn it on and off?

For most homes with a boiler and radiators, it is cheaper to turn the heating on and off so it only runs when you need it. A home loses heat continuously while it is warm, so keeping it warm for extra hours means paying to replace extra hours of lost heat. Use a timer and thermostat so the house is warm when you are in it and awake, and cooler when you are out or asleep.

Why does reheating the house not cost more in the end?

Reheating feels expensive because the boiler works hard for a while, but it is only replacing the heat the house lost while it was cooling — and a cooler house loses heat more slowly, because the gap between inside and outside temperatures is smaller. The total energy needed to reheat is less than the energy that would have been needed to hold the higher temperature for the whole period.

When does leaving heating on low make sense?

There are honest exceptions. If someone is home all day, the heating is effectively needed all day anyway. In very cold weather, a low background level protects pipes and helps prevent damp and mould from taking hold. Heat pumps are different from boilers and generally favour steady, low running rather than big on-off swings. Follow the guidance for your own system, and never let a home drop to a level where pipes could freeze.

How low can I let my home go when the heating is off?

There is no single safe number for every home, because insulation, pipe positions and weather differ. The principle is to keep a sensible frost-protection minimum in cold weather, use your boiler or thermostat's frost setting if it has one, and avoid letting rooms stay cold and damp for long periods, which encourages condensation and mould. If pipes have frozen before, or you are leaving the house empty in a cold snap, take extra care and keep background heat on.

What is the cheapest way to time heating?

Set the timer so the heating comes on shortly before you get up and before you return home, and goes off around when you leave and before bed, adjusting the lead time to how quickly your home warms up and cools down. Pair it with a thermostat so the system stops once rooms are warm enough, and use radiator valves to heat occupied rooms more than spare rooms.
