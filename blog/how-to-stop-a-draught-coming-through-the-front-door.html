How to Stop a Draught Coming Through the Front Door

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only — prices are typical examples that vary by shop and country; not a substitute for professional safety advice.

Learning how to stop a draught coming through the front door starts with one fact: a door rarely leaks everywhere at once. Usually one edge — most often the bottom — does almost all the damage, while the letterbox, keyhole or a loose corner adds smaller streams around it. Find the leaking edges first, seal them in order, and a cold hallway can change in an afternoon.

This is a practical UK-focused guide, because British front doors bring particular draught points: letterboxes through the door itself, older keyholes, and timber doors that have moved with age. Prices are typical examples only — they vary by shop and locality. For the wider picture, our list of draught fixes under $25 also covers windows, chimneys and loft hatches.

First, diagnose: which edge is actually leaking?

Do not buy a seal until you know where the air is moving. Close the door, and on a cold or windy day run the back of your hand slowly around the whole perimeter without touching it — bottom, both sides, top, letterbox and keyhole in turn. Moving air feels distinctly colder against skin, and the worst gap usually announces itself as a thin, icy line.

For smaller leaks, use the tissue test: hold a strip of tissue close to a suspect edge and watch for fluttering. A door can leak in more than one place — a bottom gap plus a tired letterbox brush is common. Our companion guide to finding draughts in your home covers the same tests room by room. Look as well as feel: daylight around a closed door is a gap, a perished frame gasket needs replacing not covering up, and an uneven gap — wide at the top, tight at the bottom — points to a door that has dropped or warped.

How to stop a draught coming through the front door: start at the bottom

The gap under the door is usually the largest single opening, and it sits where the coldest air gathers. A door snake — a weighted fabric tube laid against the closed door — is the no-tools option: roughly $8–$20 to buy, or almost nothing made from old fabric and filling. It is ideal for renters or as a tonight-only fix, but it only works while in place and does nothing for other gaps.

A brush strip screws to the bottom of the door inside, its bristles flexing over the floor; a rubber sweep does the same job with a flexible blade and suits smooth floors. Typical material cost for either is roughly $8–$20. Set it so it just touches the floor — pressed flat it wears quickly; floating above it, it seals nothing. A threshold seal, fitted to the floor for the door to close against, typically costs roughly $10–$25 in materials; check the door clears it, and consider anyone who might trip on a raised edge. Afterwards, open and close the door a dozen times: a seal that drags hard will soon be removed.

Sides and top: foam, V-strip and compression seals

For the sides and top, the right material depends on the gap. Self-adhesive foam tape sticks to the frame where the door closes against it and compresses as the door shuts — roughly $5–$12 per roll in typical material cost. Clean the frame first, because tape over dust or flaking paint peels within days, and choose a thickness that compresses slightly: too thin seals nothing, too thick means shouldering the door shut and straining the latch.

V-strip, a springy folded strip, suits narrower, even gaps on timber doors — roughly $6–$15 per pack in typical material cost. Compression seals — rubber or silicone gaskets, some slotted into a frame groove — are the longest-lasting option and what most modern doors have from new; if yours has gone flat, replacing like with like beats adding tape over it. Work one edge at a time and test the closing after each. The goal is a soft, even resistance and a click from the latch — not a door you have to lean on.

The letterbox and keyhole: small holes, real draughts

A letterbox is, by design, a hole through your door, and on a windy night it can whistle. The standard fixes are an internal brush seal, whose bristles post pushes through but air mostly cannot, or a fitted internal flap or cover — roughly $8–$18 in typical material cost. Measure the aperture before ordering, because sizes vary, and afterwards test with real post, including a stiff envelope: a seal that stops the draught but also stops or tears the post has swapped one problem for another.

The keyhole is the smallest fix and one of the most satisfying. An uncovered traditional keyhole is a tiny wind tunnel you can feel from a step away; a swinging keyhole cover typically costs roughly $3–$8 in materials and fits with one or two screws. Modern cylinder locks leak far less, but an open older keyhole takes minutes to cover.

Warped doors, hinges and latches: know where DIY ends

Sometimes the seal is not the problem — the door is. Timber doors can warp slightly with age and moisture, and any door can drop a fraction on its hinges. The signs are a gap much wider at one corner, sticking at one point and rattling at another, or a latch that no longer meets its keep cleanly. Adding thicker tape to a door that does not sit square usually just makes closing worse.

Basic checks are within most people's reach: tighten loose hinge screws, check the latch plate is firm, and look for rub marks where the door catches. Beyond that — rehanging a door, planing a twisted edge, packing hinges, refitting a latch — is joinery. If the door is warped, the frame damaged, or lock alignment is involved, call a joiner; a door that sits square makes every seal work properly. Renters vs owners, and testing your fix

Owners can choose freely from everything above. Renters should sort fixes into two groups: reversible, no-damage options such as a door snake can usually be done straight away, while anything screwed, drilled or permanently stuck down — brush strips, threshold bars, letterbox brushes, keyhole covers — should be agreed with the landlord first, ideally in writing. A badly fitting front door in a rented property is normally the landlord's responsibility to repair anyway.

Whoever you are, test the finished job the way you diagnosed it: repeat the hand and tissue test, check the door still opens, closes and latches smoothly, confirm the letterbox passes post, and make sure nothing drags or has to be forced. A sealed door stops warm air being constantly replaced by cold, so the hallway should hold its temperature noticeably better.

Safety: ventilation and fire doors are not draughts

Two safety lines matter more than any seal. First, draught-proofing means sealing unintentional gaps — never purpose-made ventilation. Do not block air bricks, trickle vents, or any vent or flue serving a boiler, fire or other fuel-burning appliance: an appliance starved of air or unable to expel fumes can produce carbon monoxide, an odourless gas that can be fatal. Keep a working carbon monoxide alarm in any room with such an appliance, and if you are unsure whether an opening is ventilation or a gap, leave it alone and ask a qualified heating engineer.

Second, take special care with fire doors. Front doors in flats, and doors on escape routes, are often fire doors that must close and latch fully, on their own, every time. Anything that stops a fire door from latching is not a comfort improvement — it is a safety failure. Never remove or disable a door closer, and if your door is or might be a fire door, get professional or landlord advice before fitting anything. Ordinary house doors, too, must still close, latch, lock and open for escape properly.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find which edge of my front door is leaking?

On a cold or windy day, run the back of your hand slowly around each edge of the closed door — bottom, sides, top, letterbox and keyhole — without touching it. Moving air feels colder against skin. A strip of tissue held near a suspect edge will flutter where air is passing, which helps you pinpoint the gap before you buy anything.

What is the best way to seal the gap under a front door?

For most doors, a brush strip or rubber sweep fitted to the bottom of the door, adjusted so it just touches the floor or threshold, gives a lasting seal. A threshold seal can help where the floor level allows it. A door snake is the no-tools option — useful for renters and internal doors — but it only works while it is in place.

Can renters draught-proof a front door?

Usually, yes, with reversible fixes: a door snake needs no fitting at all, and some self-adhesive foam seals can be removed later, though they may leave residue. Anything screwed, drilled or permanently stuck to the door or frame — brush strips, letterbox brushes, keyhole covers — should be agreed with your landlord first, and fire doors need professional or landlord handling.

Will sealing my front door cause condensation?

Sealing unintentional gaps around one door is normal draught-proofing and should not cause problems on its own. Difficulties arise when a home becomes under-ventilated overall. Never block purpose-made ventilation such as air bricks, trickle vents or flues, and keep using extractor fans and brief window opening after showers and cooking.

When should I call a joiner instead of fitting a seal?

Call a joiner if the door is warped or twisted, if the gap is much bigger on one side than the other, if hinges are loose in damaged wood, or if the latch no longer lines up with its keep. A seal cannot correct a door that does not sit square in its frame — the underlying alignment needs fixing first.
