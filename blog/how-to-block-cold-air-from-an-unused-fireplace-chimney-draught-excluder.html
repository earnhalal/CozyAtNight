How to Block Cold Air From an Unused Fireplace: Chimney Draught Excluder Guide

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. This guide is only for fireplaces and chimneys that are genuinely not in use — read the safety warnings carefully before you fit anything.

An unused chimney is a hole in your roof: draught excluders explained. It introduces this plain answer to the search "how to block cold air from an unused fireplace chimney draught excluder", because an open flue quietly drains warm air all winter long.

People searching how to block cold air from an unused fireplace chimney draught excluder options often find only sellers' pages, so this guide explains the choices plainly, without product picks.

Critical safety warning — read first. Everything in this guide is only for chimneys and fireplaces that are not in use. Always remove any excluder completely before any fire is lit. Never block a flue that serves any fuel-burning appliance, including a gas fire or boiler. Blocking a working flue can send smoke and carbon monoxide into the home, and carbon monoxide can be fatal.

Why an unused chimney pulls so much heat out

The reason is the stack effect. Warm air inside a home is lighter than cold outdoor air, so it naturally rises and looks for the highest way out. An open chimney flue gives it exactly that: a smooth vertical tube from the fireplace to the roof. Warm air flows up and out through it, and colder air is pulled in elsewhere — under doors, through gaps and down into the room around the fireplace — to replace it.

With no fire to benefit, all that movement is pure loss, which is why this fix sits naturally alongside the wider measures in our guide to keep your house warm without turning the heating up.

How to block cold air from an unused fireplace chimney draught excluder options explained

There are four broad approaches. They differ in how completely they close the flue, how easily they can be removed, and whether they belong in DIY hands at all. None of the descriptions below is a product recommendation — sizes, materials and prices vary locally, and you should follow the specific maker's or installer's guidance for your chimney.

Inflatable chimney balloon. A tough inflatable bladder is pushed a short way up the flue and pumped up until it presses gently against the flue walls and holds itself in place, with a tube left hanging down for inflation and removal. Its strength is reversibility: let the air out and the flue is open again.

Wool or felt excluder pad. A thick pad of wool, felt or similar material, often on a handle or board, is pushed up into the flue throat and wedges there. These pads are simple, contain no pump or mechanism, and can allow a small amount of air movement around their edges.

Fitted damper or cap basics. Some chimneys have, or can be fitted with, a damper inside the flue or a cap at the top that closes the opening. A top cap is work at roof height and belongs with a professional.

Boarding or a register plate. As a more permanent option, the fireplace opening or flue throat can be boarded over or fitted with a metal register plate by a professional. This is the neatest long-term answer for a fireplace that is permanently out of use, and it is also the option that most needs proper ventilation planning and building advice, because the closed flue still needs to be managed correctly over time.

What the materials typically cost

As typical examples only — and local prices vary widely by size, material and country — simple wool or felt pads and basic inflatable excluders are usually modest DIY purchases, often in the low tens of pounds or dollars, while professional work such as a fitted cap, boarding or a register plate costs considerably more because it includes labour and roof or building work.

Measuring the flue before you choose anything

Flues are rarely the tidy rectangle people expect. Measure the inside width and depth just above the fireplace opening, at the height where an excluder would sit, following the measuring method supplied for the type you are considering. Measure in more than one place, because many flues narrow, step inwards or begin to bend shortly above the opening.

Before measuring, make sure the fireplace is cold, has not been used recently, and is genuinely out of use. Wear gloves and eye protection, expect soot, and protect the floor and furnishings.

Fitting, in general terms

The exact steps depend on the type of excluder and its maker's instructions, which always override a general guide like this one. In outline: clean loose soot from the fitting area, check the excluder for damage, position it at the directed height inside the flue — high enough to be out of the way of the opening, low enough to retrieve safely — and secure or inflate it only as far as the instructions direct.

Work slowly and never force anything up a flue. If an excluder will not go in, will not stay, or disappears further up than intended, stop and reassess rather than pushing harder. The visible-reminder rule

Never rely on memory. Attach the tag supplied with your excluder, or leave a clear note on the fireplace itself, so that nobody — family, guests, a new tenant or a future owner — lights a fire without knowing the flue is blocked. Always remove any excluder before any fire, every time, without exception.

This rule deserves its own section because it is where a safe, sensible fix could otherwise become dangerous months later, when the excluder is out of sight and forgotten. Tell every adult in the household that it is fitted and where the reminder is. If the property is sold or let, pass that information on explicitly. An excluder that nobody knows about is a hazard; an excluder with a visible tag and an informed household is a managed, reversible draught fix.

The ventilation nuance: why a total seal can be wrong

It is tempting to aim for a completely airtight block, but many installers deliberately leave a small vent path when closing an unused flue. A chimney is a tall, cool column of masonry, and warm moist air that gets trapped inside it with nowhere to go can condense and leave the flue damp, which over time can show as staining or damage.

That is why the right answer is to follow the guidance supplied with your excluder, or an installer's advice for your specific chimney, rather than adding extra tape, foam or sealant to chase a perfect seal. Reducing a strong cold draught and keeping a flue healthy are both the goal — one should not be bought at the expense of the other.

When not to do it yourself

Do not fit an excluder yourself if the flue or chimney breast is damaged, cracked or damp; if the flue is shared with another fireplace or appliance; if there is any doubt at all about whether the fireplace is truly and permanently out of use; or if other people in the home might light a fire without understanding the block. Above all, any chimney connected in any way to a gas appliance is a qualified-professional-only job — never block, cap or alter it yourself. Carbon monoxide can be fatal, and it gives no warning smell.

In those cases the correct next step is a qualified chimney sweep, heating engineer or builder who can inspect the flue and advise. For the smaller gaps elsewhere in the house, our round-up of 10 draught fixes under $25 covers the cheaper, simpler jobs you can do the same afternoon.

Frequently asked questions

Can you block an unused chimney completely?

Many installers leave a small ventilation path rather than sealing a flue completely, because a fully sealed flue can trap moist air and lead to damp inside the chimney. Follow the guidance supplied with your excluder, or an installer's advice for your chimney, instead of aiming for a total seal.

Do you have to remove a chimney draught excluder before lighting a fire?

Yes, always. Remove any excluder completely before any fire is lit, without exception, and make sure every adult in the home knows it is there. Never block a flue that serves any fuel-burning appliance, and never light a fire under a fitted excluder — smoke and carbon monoxide can be fatal.

How do you know what size chimney excluder you need?

Measure the inside of the flue, usually just above the fireplace opening where the excluder will sit, following the maker's measuring guidance. Flues vary widely in width, depth and shape, and may narrow or bend, so measure carefully in more than one place before choosing any option.

Is it safe to fit a chimney draught excluder yourself?

It can be a straightforward job for a sound, genuinely unused open fireplace, but not every chimney is suitable for DIY. Do not do it yourself if the flue is damaged or shared, if there is any doubt the fireplace is truly out of use, or if any gas appliance is involved — use a qualified professional instead.

Will blocking an unused chimney make a room feel warmer?

It can make a noticeable difference to draughts and to the feeling of cold air falling into the room, because an open flue constantly pulls warm air out and lets cold air replace it. It will not add heat, so it works best alongside other draught-proofing around doors and windows.
