What Tog Duvet Do I Need for Winter? A Room-Temperature Guide

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only. Tog is the standard UK duvet warmth labelling system — use this guide as a starting point, not a rule for every sleeper.

If you are asking what tog duvet do I need for winter, the short answer for most UK homes is 13.5 tog — the standard winter weight. That answer changes with your bedroom, though. A well-heated, modern bedroom may be comfortable with 10.5 tog all winter, while an unheated or draughty room may need 13.5 tog plus a layer, or a 15 tog duvet on the coldest nights. This guide works from your room temperature outwards.

Tog is not a quality score. It is simply the UK labelling system for how warm a duvet is intended to feel, with higher numbers meaning warmer duvets. For the room side of the equation, see our guide to the best bedroom temperature for sleep in winter.

What tog duvet do I need for winter? Start with your bedroom temperature

Before you look at labels, look at the room at bedtime. A simple thermometer on the bedside table, away from the radiator and the window, tells you more than the hallway thermostat. Note the temperature for a few nights alongside how you slept: waking chilled points one way, waking hot and kicking the duvet off points the other. Most adults sleep best in a cool rather than warm room; the duvet makes the bed itself warm inside it.

The UK tog scale explained

UK duvets are sold in a small set of standard tog ratings. A 4.5 tog duvet is the light summer weight. Ratings around 7.5 tog and 9 tog are light to mid weights, useful in spring, autumn and warmer bedrooms. A 10.5 tog duvet is the common mid weight. A 13.5 tog duvet is the standard winter weight, and 15 tog duvets exist for extra-warm needs and very cold rooms. You are choosing a band of warmth, not a precise temperature.

There is no single correct winter tog, only a sensible starting band for your room — and the same duvet can feel very different in a heated flat and in an older house with single-glazed windows.

Tog by bedroom temperature: a simple chart

Use this chart as a starting point, then adjust after a few nights. It assumes ordinary pyjamas and no extra blanket unless the row says otherwise.

Treat the temperatures as rough guides, not thresholds. If you sit between two rows, choose the lower tog and keep a blanket within reach: it is easier to add a layer at 2am than to cool down under a duvet that is too warm. Heated bedrooms versus unheated and draughty rooms

In a heated bedroom, the risk is usually too much warmth, not too little. Central heating that runs into the evening can hold a bedroom warm for hours, and a 13.5 tog duvet on top can leave you waking hot. If you regularly kick the duvet off in a heated home, step down to 10.5 tog, and turn the bedroom radiator down rather than opening a window onto heat you have already paid for.

In an unheated or draughty bedroom, cold moving air is the real enemy. A higher tog helps, but it cannot fix a draught across the pillow or an icy bed at lights-out. Seal the gaps you can, pre-warm the bed, and then let a 13.5 or 15 tog duvet hold that warmth. All-season duvets: two duvets in one

An all-season duvet is a pair: usually a lighter 4.5 tog duvet and a mid-weight 9 tog duvet that fasten together. Used separately, they cover summer and the in-between seasons; joined, they are intended to give roughly 13.5 tog of winter warmth. The appeal is flexibility and storage — one system for the whole year instead of a winter duvet that lives in a cupboard for months. The joined pair can feel bulkier than a single 13.5 tog duvet of similar warmth.

When two sleepers want different warmth

One duvet is always a compromise when one of you runs hot and the other feels every draught. Rather than meeting in a middle that suits neither, separate the warmth. Two single duvets on one bed let each person choose their own tog and add or shed their own covers. A shared duvet with an extra blanket folded on the colder side works almost as well. Our comparison of two thin blankets or one thick duvet, and which is warmer explains when separate layers beat one shared cover.

Spare rooms and guest beds

Guests feel your spare room differently from you, and they will rarely say anything until morning. A sensible spare-room default is 10.5 tog in a heated home or 13.5 tog in a colder one, with a clean blanket folded at the foot of the bed so guests can add warmth without asking. That combination covers most visitors in most weeks of winter. In a cold snap, that extra blanket matters more than a second, heavier duvet.

Fillings, loft and drape: why the same tog can feel different

Tog describes intended warmth, not feel. Two duvets with the same tog can behave quite differently in bed, largely because of loft and drape. Loft is how much the filling puffs up and traps still air inside the duvet. A high-loft duvet tends to feel light for its warmth, because the warmth comes from trapped air rather than from weight resting on you.

Loft, drape and cold gaps

Drape is how closely the duvet follows the shape of your body. A duvet that drapes well leaves fewer cold gaps at your shoulders and hips, so it can feel warmer in practice, especially in a draughty room. A stiffer duvet can tent over you and let cooled air in at the edges. Use tog to choose the warmth band, then expect loft and drape to decide how that warmth feels.

Babies and young children: a separate rule

Do not apply this adult chart to babies or young children. Their bedding, room temperature and sleep position are covered by official safe-sleep guidance, and overheating is a specific concern for infants. Follow the current guidance for your country and your child's age, and ask your midwife, health visitor or clinician if you are unsure. Adult tog ratings are a labelling system for adult duvets, not a scale to shrink down for a cot or a child's bed.

A simple way to choose, then adjust

If you want the decision in one pass, work through these steps in order and stop when the bed feels right for a few nights running.

  • Measure the room: note the bedroom temperature at bedtime for two or three nights.
  • Pick the starting tog: use the chart above for that temperature.
  • Keep a layer in reach: one removable blanket for the coldest nights.
  • Change one thing at a time: tog, layers or radiator setting — then note the result.
  • Revisit when the season turns: not every night.

Most people end up with a sensible tog for their room, one extra layer in reach, and a radiator setting that does not fight the bedding — a bed you stop thinking about.

Frequently asked questions

What tog duvet do I need for winter?

For most heated UK bedrooms, 13.5 tog is the standard winter choice, while a milder or well-heated room may only need 10.5 tog. If your bedroom is unheated, draughty or often very cold at night, 13.5 tog with a removable extra layer, or a 15 tog duvet, may suit better. Start from your room temperature rather than the label alone, and adjust after a few nights.

Is 10.5 tog warm enough for winter?

It can be, especially in a modern, well-insulated or centrally heated bedroom that stays near the warmer end of a comfortable sleeping range. In a colder, older or draughty home, 10.5 tog often feels light by midwinter unless you add a blanket on top. Many people use 10.5 tog in autumn and spring, then switch up when nights turn colder.

What is an all-season duvet?

An all-season duvet is a pair of duvets, usually a lighter 4.5 tog and a mid-weight 9 tog, used separately in warmer months or joined together to make roughly a 13.5 tog winter duvet. It suits homes where bedroom temperature changes a lot through the year, and it avoids storing a separate winter duvet.

What if one partner runs hot and the other feels cold?

Separate the warmth rather than compromising on one duvet that suits neither of you. Two single duvets on a shared bed, different layers on each side, or an extra blanket on the colder side all let each sleeper adjust independently. Changing the bedding is usually easier and cheaper than changing the temperature of the whole room for one person.

What tog duvet should babies and young children use?

Do not use this adult guide for babies or young children. Follow the current official safe-sleep guidance for your country and your child's age for bedding, room temperature and sleep position, and avoid overheating. If you are unsure what bedding is suitable, ask your midwife, health visitor or clinician rather than scaling down an adult tog choice.
