Why Can't I Sleep in Winter? Common Causes and Practical Fixes

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only, not medical advice and not a diagnosis. If poor sleep, low mood or breathing problems persist or are severe, please talk to a clinician.

If you keep asking why can't I sleep in winter, you are not imagining it: the season changes almost everything around sleep at once. Days get shorter, indoor air gets drier, bedrooms swing between cold and stuffy, and routines around food, drink and screens tend to drift later. Any one of those can fragment a night. Put together, they explain why a person who sleeps fine in June can lie awake in January.

This guide works through the most common winter causes one by one, each with practical fixes to try at home. None of this is a diagnosis — so the red flags at the end matter as much as the tips. Start with whichever cause sounds most like your nights.

Why can't I sleep in winter? Start with daylight

Your body clock is set largely by light, especially early in the day. In winter, sunrise is later, days are shorter, and many people travel to and from work or school in the dark. With less daylight, the clock can drift: sleepy at odd times, wide awake at bedtime, groggy well into the morning. Irregular weekend lie-ins push the drift further.

Practical fixes: keep your wake time as consistent as you can, even on days off, because a steady wake time anchors the whole day. Get outside in the morning if you can — even a short walk in ordinary winter daylight exposes you to far more light than indoor lighting. If mornings are not possible, take daylight whenever the day allows, such as a lunchtime walk. This is also where seasonal low mood needs a careful mention: many people feel flatter in winter, and low mood and poor sleep often arrive together. This article cannot diagnose seasonal affective disorder or any other condition. If low mood persists, deepens, or affects your daily life, talk to a clinician — that is a health conversation, not a home-tips conversation.

Dry heated air: the blocked nose and dry throat problem

Heating a home for months dries the indoor air, and you breathe it all night. A dry nose and throat, a congested feeling, or waking up needing water are common winter complaints, and each small waking makes sleep lighter. It is often worst in bedrooms kept warm all night, where closed doors concentrate the effect.

Practical fixes: keep a glass of water by the bed so a dry throat does not mean a full wake-up trip to the kitchen. Try a slightly lower bedroom temperature, which helps both dryness and sleep in general. If dryness is a nightly issue in your home, our full guide to using a humidifier in winter, including cleaning and placement explains when one can help and how to look after it properly — a poorly maintained humidifier is not an improvement, so the cleaning advice is part of the fix, not an afterthought.

A bedroom that is too cold, too hot — or a bed that starts out icy

Temperature cuts both ways in winter. A bedroom heated hard against the cold can stay stuffy past midnight, while an unheated or draughty room can leave you curled up and tense. A bed that is cold when you get into it is its own problem: you lie awake waiting to warm up, and tense, cold muscles do not help.

For the room itself, our dedicated guide to the best bedroom temperature for sleep in winter, including the commonly cited range explains why a cooler room with warm bedding suits many adults, and how to measure your own room properly. For a cold bed, pre-warming is the targeted fix: warm the bed for a short time before you get in, with layered bedding ready to hold that warmth. If you are considering an electric blanket for that job, read our guide to choosing an electric blanket with the right controls and safety features before you buy, and always follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Heavier, later routines: food, alcohol, caffeine and screens

Winter routines drift. Holidays bring bigger, later meals; cold evenings encourage staying in, snacking and streaming later. Some of that works directly against sleep. A large meal close to bedtime can leave you uncomfortable. Alcohol can make you feel sleepy while fragmenting sleep later — falling asleep quickly, then waking in the early hours. Caffeine late in the day can still be in your system at bedtime. This is general information, not a rule about any one person's sensitivity.

Practical fixes: keep evening meals earlier and lighter where you can, and notice whether nights after alcohol are more broken — many people find they are. Move your last caffeine toward morning or early afternoon, and give screens a natural stopping point before bed. Small, consistent changes beat a strict regime you abandon by Friday.

Less movement in the day

Cold, dark weather quietly removes the activity built into the day: walking to places, gardening, being outside. Less movement can mean arriving at bedtime less physically tired, while your mind is just as busy. Practical fixes: build gentle daytime activity back in, in whatever form fits your life and health — a daylight walk does double duty, adding movement and light. Indoor options count too. If you have a health condition or have been inactive for a long time, check with a clinician before starting anything strenuous.

Clock changes and irregular schedules

Clock changes can throw sleep off for days, and winter adds irregularity: holidays, visitors, late nights and long lie-ins in a short stretch. Your body clock handles a single late night easily; it struggles with a different schedule every day. Practical fixes: after a clock change or run of late nights, return to your usual wake time first, rather than forcing an early bedtime while you are not sleepy. Morning regularity tends to pull bedtime back into line within a few days.

A one-week reset you can actually follow

Do not try to fix everything tonight. Use a week to reset the basics, one layer at a time:

  1. Days 1–2: fix your wake time and keep it, and get outside for daylight soon after waking.
  2. Days 2–3: set the bedroom cooler, layer the bedding, and pre-warm a cold bed before you get in.
  3. Day 3: ease dry air — water by the bed, lower bedroom heat, and consider the humidifier guidance above if dryness is nightly.
  4. Days 4–5: move caffeine earlier, make the evening meal lighter or earlier, and notice the effect of alcohol on your second half of the night.
  5. Days 5–6: add a gentle daytime walk or other activity, ideally in daylight.
  6. Day 7: review honestly. Which two changes helped most? Keep those, drop the rest, and repeat for another week before judging.

A short morning note — slept well, woke hot, nose dry, late meal — turns a vague feeling into a pattern you can act on.

Red flags: when poor winter sleep needs a clinician

Some sleep problems are not home-fix problems. Talk to a clinician if you notice any of the following, rather than relying on general articles like this one:

  • Loud, regular snoring, or pauses in breathing during sleep that a partner notices
  • Insomnia that persists for weeks despite steadier routines
  • Severe daytime sleepiness, especially if it affects driving, work or safety
  • Low mood that persists or deepens through the winter
  • Heavy night sweating, chest pain, or breathing difficulty at night

These can be signs of conditions that need proper assessment and, where appropriate, treatment. This article is general information only: it is not medical advice, it does not diagnose anything, and it cannot replace a clinician who knows you.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I sleep worse in winter?

Winter combines several things that can fragment sleep: shorter days and less morning light can shift your body clock, heated indoor air can dry your nose and throat, bedrooms can be too cold or too hot, and routines often become heavier and less regular. No single cause explains every case, which is why working through light, air, temperature and routine one at a time is more useful than one big change.

Can dry air keep you awake at night?

It can contribute. Heated indoor air often feels dry in winter, and a dry nose, throat or congested feeling can make it harder to settle and can wake you during the night. A glass of water by the bed, a slightly lower bedroom temperature and, where dryness is persistent, a properly maintained humidifier may help. If congestion or breathing difficulty is severe or ongoing, see a clinician.

Does cold weather affect sleep?

Cold weather itself matters less than what it does to your bedroom and your day: a cold or draughty bed can keep you awake, while a room that is overheated against the cold can do the same. Cold, dark days also tend to reduce daylight and daytime activity, both of which support sleep. Keeping the bedroom comfortably cool, the bed warm, and your days as light and active as you can usually helps more than any single gadget.

How can I sleep better in winter?

Start with the basics for a week: keep a consistent wake time, get outdoor light early in the day, keep the bedroom cool with warm, layered bedding, ease dry air, cut caffeine earlier in the day, and keep evenings and meals more regular. Change a few things at a time and note what helps. The one-week reset in this article lays that out day by day.

When should I see a doctor about poor sleep?

See a clinician if poor sleep persists for weeks, if you snore loudly or have pauses in breathing during sleep, if daytime sleepiness is severe or affects driving or work, or if low mood continues. Those can be signs of a condition that needs proper assessment. This article is general information only and is not medical advice, a diagnosis or a treatment.
