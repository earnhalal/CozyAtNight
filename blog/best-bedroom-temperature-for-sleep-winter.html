What Is the Best Bedroom Temperature for Sleep in Winter?

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General information only, not medical advice. Temperature ranges below are commonly cited general guidance, not prescriptions — see a clinician for persistent or severe sleep problems.

If you are trying to find the best bedroom temperature for sleep, the short answer most sleep sources give for adults is a cool room, not a warm one: roughly 16–19°C (60–67°F), with around 18°C (65°F) as a sensible starting point. That is general guidance, not a rule for every body, every home or every night. The useful question is not which single number is perfect, but how to get your room into that kind of range — and then adjust your bedding, rather than the heating, as the night changes.

Winter makes this harder than summer. Central heating can leave bedrooms stuffy long after bedtime, while a draughty room can drop cold enough to wake you at 3am. This guide explains why a cooler room helps, and how bedding, age and a partner who disagrees change the answer.

What is the best bedroom temperature for sleep for most adults?

The commonly cited adult range is roughly 16–19°C (60–67°F). Many sleep sources suggest starting around 18°C or 65°F and then moving a degree or two in either direction based on how you actually sleep for a few nights. Treat the range as a starting zone to test, not a prescription. Some people settle happily at the cooler end; others sleep better a little warmer, especially if they feel the cold easily.

Watch the pattern instead of chasing a number. Waking hot and kicking the duvet off suggests you are too warm; waking curled tight with cold feet or shoulders suggests too cold or draughty. Adjust one thing at a time — room first, then bedding.

Why a cool room helps: your body cools down for sleep

Sleep is linked to a natural fall in core body temperature in the evening. As you get sleepy, your body begins to shed heat, particularly through your hands and feet, and that cooling helps signal that it is time to sleep. A bedroom that is too warm works against that process: your body has to work harder to lose heat, and you may take longer to fall asleep or wake more often feeling hot and restless.

That is why a cool room plus warm bedding suits many people better than a warm room plus light bedding: the air around your face stays cool, while the duvet keeps your body warm. Warm feet help too — loose bed socks are a cheap fix for cold feet. If overheating is part of a wider pattern of broken nights, our guide to why you might sleep worse in winter and what to try covers the other common causes.

The winter paradox: bedrooms that are too hot and bedrooms that are too cold

Winter creates two opposite problems, sometimes in the same house. Where central heating runs into the evening, bedrooms can still be warm at midnight, insulation holds the heat, and the sleeper wakes up hot. Turning the heating off earlier, or just the bedroom radiator down, often fixes it.

At the other extreme are cold, draughty bedrooms: single-glazed windows, gaps around frames, rooms above unheated spaces. Here the problem is cold air moving across the bed, or a bed that starts out icy. Pre-warm the bed, seal draughts and layer bedding — heating the whole room hard all night is the most expensive, least targeted fix.

Bedding and tog: warmth you can adjust

Bedding is your fine control; room temperature is the coarse one. Get the room roughly right, then tune the bed. Layers — a duvet plus a blanket you can add, remove or fold back — give you several settings instead of one fixed, very warm cover.

What tog means, for US readers

In the UK, duvet warmth is measured in togs. A low tog, around 4.5, is a light summer duvet. A high tog, around 13.5, is a warm winter duvet, with mid-weight options in between. The US does not use a single standard rating in the same way, so think of tog simply as a warmth scale: higher number, warmer duvet. The right winter tog depends on your room. A well-heated bedroom may only need a mid-weight duvet; a cold room may need a higher tog or the same duvet plus a layer on top.

Small adjustments that make a real difference

  • Warm feet, cool face: loose socks if your feet run cold; keep your head and the air you breathe cooler.
  • Layers over one heavy cover: a blanket over the duvet on the coldest nights, removed when the room is milder.
  • Breathable pyjamas: very thick sleepwear under a warm duvet is a common cause of waking up hot.
  • Pre-warm a cold bed, not a cold room: warming the bed itself for a short time before you get in costs far less than heating the room for hours.

Different sleepers, different needs

Older adults

Older adults often prefer a slightly warmer room and may feel the cold more. UK-style general guidance for older and more vulnerable people stresses keeping living spaces and bedrooms adequately warm in cold weather, rather than letting rooms become cold — it is about avoiding cold homes, not one exact sleeping temperature. If you care for someone older or unwell, follow current local guidance and their clinician's advice.

Babies and children

For babies and young children, follow official guidance rather than general articles. Avoid overheating, and use the current safe-sleep guidance for your country for room temperature, bedding and sleep position for your child's age. Do not add loose bedding based on tips aimed at adults. If unsure, ask your clinician, midwife or health visitor.

Partners who disagree

Two people in one bed often want two different temperatures. Separate duvets are the simplest fix: each person controls their own warmth. A dual-control electric blanket is another option, with a different setting on each side — our guide to how to choose an electric blanket safely and in the right size explains the controls and safety checks to look for. Solving it in the bedding, not the room, also keeps costs down.

How to measure your bedroom temperature properly

Do not judge by the hallway thermostat: it measures that spot, not your bedroom at pillow height. Put a simple room thermometer on a bedside table or shelf, away from the radiator, a cold window and direct draughts. Check it at bedtime and note it alongside how you felt. A few nights of that pairing will show your own comfortable range inside the commonly cited one.

Dry air can distort the picture too — a comfortable temperature can still feel harsh on nose and throat — which is why our winter humidifier guide for dry indoor air is worth reading if you wake up congested.

Keeping costs down while cooling a bedroom

Cooling a bedroom should not waste heat. If it is too warm at night, turn its radiator or thermostatic radiator valve (TRV) down rather than opening a window while the heating is on — a window throws away heat you have already paid for, while turning the radiator down earlier stops that heat being made. With no separate control, lowering the main thermostat a little overnight, where safe for everyone at home, is the next option.

For cold bedrooms, warm the person and the bed before the whole room. Our guides to the cheapest ways to heat a room at night and electric blanket versus space heater running costs show why low-wattage bed warming usually costs far less than heating air all night.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should a bedroom be at night in winter?

For many adults, sleep sources commonly cite roughly 16–19°C (60–67°F) as a comfortable bedroom range in winter, with around 18°C (65°F) as a practical starting point. Treat that as general guidance, not a prescription: adjust within the range until you fall asleep without feeling chilled or overheated, and use bedding you can add or remove during the night.

Is 65°F too cold to sleep?

For most healthy adults, 65°F (about 18°C) is not too cold to sleep and sits near the middle of the range commonly cited for bedrooms. Whether it feels right depends on your bedding, pyjamas and personal preference. If you wake up chilled, add a layer or raise the room slightly; if you wake up hot and restless, try the lower end of the range instead.

Why do I wake up hot at night in winter?

Common winter reasons include central heating that keeps running after bedtime, a duvet that is too warm for the room, and bedding or pyjamas that trap heat. Your body also releases heat as it prepares for sleep, so a room that felt fine at 9pm can feel stuffy by 2am. Turning the bedroom radiator down, lowering the duvet tog, or using layers you can remove usually helps. If heavy night sweating happens often, talk to a clinician.

What tog duvet do I need for winter?

In the UK system, winter duvets are often around 13.5 tog, while lighter summer duvets are around 4.5 tog. The right choice depends on how warm your bedroom runs: a heated room may only need a mid-weight duvet, while a cold, draughty room may need the higher tog or extra layers. Layering a thinner duvet with a blanket you can remove is more flexible than one very warm duvet.

Should a baby's bedroom be warmer?

Do not guess with babies and young children. Avoid overheating, follow the current safe-sleep guidance for your country, and check official advice on room temperature, bedding and sleep position for your child's age. This article is general information for adults and cannot replace that official guidance or advice from your own clinician or health visitor.
