Do Electric Blankets Use a Lot of Electricity? The Real Numbers

CozyAtNight · 28 October 2026 · General guidance only. Electricity prices vary widely by country, state, provider and tariff — every rate below is clearly labelled as illustrative, and we show you how to calculate your own.

If you are asking do electric blankets use a lot of electricity, the short answer is no. A typical electric blanket uses roughly 50 to 150 watts — about the same range as a bright old light bulb, and a small fraction of what a room heater draws. Even run for a full eight-hour night at its maximum setting, most blankets use well under 1 kWh in total. This guide shows the real numbers per hour, per night and per month, in both US and UK rates, and how to work out the cost of your own blanket in about a minute.

That is why blankets appear in any honest look at the cheapest way to heat a room at night: they put heat where you are lying, under covers that help hold it in, instead of warming all the air in a bedroom.

Do electric blankets use a lot of electricity?

Compared with almost any other electric heating appliance, no. The key number is wattage, which tells you how fast an appliance uses electricity while it is on. A blanket at 50 to 150 watts sits at the very low end of household heating. It warms a bed and a body at close contact, not the air in a whole room, so it does not need much power to do its job.

Electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours, or kWh — one kilowatt used for one hour. A 100W blanket is 0.1 kilowatts, so an eight-hour night at full power is 0.8 kWh. A 60W blanket uses 0.48 kWh over the same night, and a 150W model 1.2 kWh if it stayed at full power throughout, which in practice it rarely does.

The one-minute formula for any blanket

Every cost here comes from one formula: cost = kW × price per kWh, for each hour of use. Divide the wattage by 1,000 to get kilowatts. A 100W blanket is 0.10 kW, so at 16 cents per kWh it costs 0.10 × 16¢ = 1.6¢ per hour at full power. Multiply by hours for a night, and by nights for a month.

Why the label figure is a ceiling, not your bill

The tables assume full power for every minute, and that honesty matters. The wattage on the label is the maximum the blanket can draw, not what it draws all night. Once the bed reaches the temperature you chose, the thermostat cycles the elements on and off to hold it there. On a low or medium setting, the blanket may be actively drawing power for only part of each hour.

Real use on low therefore costs less than the table — sometimes noticeably less on a mild night or under a warm duvet. Treat the figures as a ceiling for planning, not a prediction. A blanket run on high in a very cold room will sit closer to that ceiling, because its thermostat switches off less often.

Dual controls, two sides and the pre-heat habit

One common surprise is the dual-control blanket. These have two heating zones and two controllers, one for each side of a shared bed, so two people can choose different temperatures. If both sides are switched on, the total draw can be roughly double the figure for a single side. Some blankets already show both zones in their headline wattage, so read the label carefully — the section below explains where to look.

The habits are simple. Switch off a side nobody is sleeping on. Pre-warm on a higher setting, then turn it down once you get in. Use any timer offered, so the blanket is not heating an empty bed in the morning.

How a blanket compares with a heater and a kettle

Watts make the comparison plain. A typical space heater draws 1,500W at full power: ten times a 150W blanket, fifteen times a 100W blanket, and twenty-five times a 60W blanket, for every hour both run. Our side-by-side look at electric blanket vs space heater costs works through what that gap means over a whole night.

A kettle labelled at around 3,000W uses about twenty times the power of a 150W blanket while it boils — but only for a few minutes, so its total energy stays small. Wattage alone is not a bill; what you pay for is watts multiplied by time. A blanket wins because even hours of its low wattage still add up to well under a kilowatt-hour on most nights.

When the saving is real — and when it disappears

A cheap-to-run blanket only saves money overall if it changes something else. The saving exists when a warm bed lets you turn the main heating down overnight, or off earlier in the evening, and let bedrooms sit cooler while you sleep comfortably. Heating one person in one bed for a few cents or pence, instead of the whole house, is where the real difference comes from.

If the main heating stays at the same setting all night and the blanket is simply added on top, there is no saving — only a small extra cost and a warmer bed. That may still be worth it for comfort, but count it honestly as a comfort cost, not a saving. Before assuming a bed blanket belongs elsewhere, read whether you can use an electric blanket on the sofa, and follow the manufacturer's intended use.

How to read your own label and work out your real cost

First, find the wattage. It is usually printed on the woven label sewn to the blanket, on the care label, on the back of the controller, or in the instruction booklet — a number followed by W, such as 60W, 100W or 150W. On a dual-control model, check whether it covers one side or both combined.

Second, find your price per kWh: the per-kWh line on a US bill, or the unit rate on a UK bill or tariff summary. Third, do the sum: watts divided by 1,000, multiplied by hours, multiplied by that rate. A 100W blanket for eight hours at 16¢ is 0.10 × 8 × 16¢ = 12.8¢; at 24p it is 19.2p. Frequently asked questions

Do electric blankets use a lot of electricity compared with a heater?

No. A typical electric blanket uses roughly 50 to 150 watts, while a typical space heater uses 1,500 watts at full power. That means the heater draws about ten times as much as a 150W blanket, and about fifteen times as much as a 100W blanket, for every hour both are switched on.

How much does an electric blanket cost to run for one night?

A 100W blanket run for eight hours uses 0.8 kWh. At an illustrative US rate of 16 cents per kWh that is 12.8 cents for the night, and at an illustrative UK rate of 24p per kWh it is 19.2p. On a low setting with the thermostat cycling, the real cost is usually lower than that full-power figure.

Is it cheaper to run an electric blanket on low all night?

Usually, yes, compared with running it on high. The wattage on the label is the maximum draw. On a lower setting the heating elements cycle on and off to hold a gentler temperature, so the blanket is not drawing full power for the whole night and the average cost falls.

Do dual controls use twice as much electricity?

They can. A dual-control blanket has two separate heating zones, one for each side of the bed. If both sides are switched on, the total draw can be roughly double the figure for one side. If only one person is in bed, switching off the unused side avoids paying for heat nobody is using.

How do I work out what my own electric blanket costs to run?

Find the wattage on the blanket label, the care label or the control, divide it by 1,000 to get kilowatts, then multiply by the hours you use it and by the price per kWh on your electricity bill. That gives your cost for that period at full power, before any saving from the thermostat cycling on lower settings.
